Part 1 — Big Picture

Origin and insertion: how a muscle pulls a bone

Every skeletal muscle attaches to bone at two points. The origin is the fixed, typically proximal attachment point that stays relatively still. The insertion is the mobile, typically distal attachment point that gets pulled when the muscle contracts. Knowing which end is which is the foundation for understanding every muscle action covered this week.

Part 1 — Structure & Function

Muscles work in functional pairs

Muscles can only pull, never push — so producing opposite movements at a joint requires opposing muscle pairs. The agonist (prime mover) produces the main movement being described; the antagonist relaxes and stretches to allow that movement, then can reverse it. A synergist (not pictured) is a third muscle that assists the agonist or stabilizes the joint during the movement.

Part 1 — Structure & Function

Five common muscle naming conventions

Muscle names aren't random — they're built from a small set of naming conventions describing the muscle's location, shape, action, relative size, or number of origins. Once you recognize these patterns, an unfamiliar muscle name often tells you most of what you need to know about it without memorizing it directly.

Part 1 — Your Turn

Apply it before we move to review

A muscle called the "extensor digitorum longus" is being described in a lab practical.

Question 1 — Based on its name alone, what movement does this muscle most likely produce?

The name directly tells you: "extensor" indicates extension, and "digitorum" refers to the digits (fingers or toes). This is a direct application of the naming-by-action convention covered this week.

Question 2 — During this extension movement, what role does this muscle play?

Since this muscle is actively producing the extension movement being described, it's acting as the agonist (prime mover) for that specific action. It would only be called the antagonist if a different muscle (like a flexor) were the one actively contracting instead.

Part 1 — Clinical Correlation

Muscle strains and the agonist/antagonist balance

This connects the functional-pair concept directly to one of the most common sports injuries.

🩺 In practice

A muscle strain occurs when a muscle is stretched or torn beyond its normal range — often when the antagonist contracts unexpectedly or too forcefully while the agonist is still lengthening. Hamstring strains are a classic example: the hamstrings (antagonist to the quadriceps during knee extension) are frequently injured during activities requiring rapid, forceful direction changes, exactly where the agonist/antagonist balance is tested hardest.

Comprehensive Practice Quiz

Exam 4 dress rehearsal — 10 questions across Ch. 10–11

Question 1
Which muscle type is voluntary and striated?
Skeletal muscle is voluntary and striated. Cardiac muscle is also striated but involuntary; smooth muscle is neither voluntary nor striated.
Question 2
A sarcomere runs from one Z-disc to the:
A sarcomere is defined as the region between two Z-discs. Fascicles and a muscle's origin are much larger structures, unrelated to the sarcomere's specific boundaries.
Question 3
Which protein makes up the thick filaments?
Myosin makes up the thick filaments. Actin makes up the thin filaments instead, and collagen is unrelated to the sarcomere's contractile proteins.
Question 4
The fixed, typically proximal attachment point of a muscle is called the:
The origin is the fixed attachment point. The insertion is the mobile end that gets pulled, and a sarcomere is a microscopic contractile unit, unrelated to whole-muscle attachment points.
Question 5
During elbow flexion, the biceps brachii acts as the:
The biceps brachii is the agonist (prime mover) for elbow flexion — it's the muscle actively producing that movement. The triceps brachii would be the antagonist in this scenario.
Question 6
A muscle named "gluteus maximus" is named according to which convention?
"Maximus" indicates size — this is the largest of the three gluteal muscles. Shape-based names (like deltoid) and origin-count names (like biceps) follow different conventions.
Question 7
Calcium's role in muscle contraction is to:
Calcium exposes the myosin-binding sites on actin, allowing contraction to begin. ATP (not calcium) provides the energy for the power stroke, and neither is involved in anchoring a muscle to bone.
Question 8
A synergist muscle's role is to:
A synergist assists the agonist or stabilizes the joint during a movement. Producing the opposite movement describes the antagonist instead, and a synergist works alongside the agonist rather than replacing it.
Question 9
"Flexor carpi radialis" is named using which combination of conventions?
"Flexor" describes the action (flexion), and "carpi radialis" describes the location (wrist, radius side) — a combination of two naming conventions, not shape/size or origin count alone.
Question 10
Rigor mortis is a direct illustration of ATP's role in muscle:
Rigor mortis shows ATP's role in relaxation — without ATP, myosin can't release actin, leaving the muscle locked in contraction. It has nothing to do with muscle growth or the naming conventions covered in Chapter 11.
