Part 1 — Big Picture

Origin and insertion: how a muscle pulls a bone

Every skeletal muscle attaches to bone at two points. The origin is the fixed, typically proximal attachment point that stays relatively still. The insertion is the mobile, typically distal attachment point that gets pulled when the muscle contracts. Knowing which end is which is the foundation for understanding every muscle action covered this week.

Part 1 — Structure & Function

Muscles work in functional pairs

Muscles can only pull, never push — so producing opposite movements at a joint requires opposing muscle pairs. The agonist (prime mover) produces the main movement being described; the antagonist relaxes and stretches to allow that movement, then can reverse it. A synergist (not pictured) is a third muscle that assists the agonist or stabilizes the joint during the movement.

Part 1 — Structure & Function

Five common muscle naming conventions

Muscle names aren't random — they're built from a small set of naming conventions describing the muscle's location, shape, action, relative size, or number of origins. Once you recognize these patterns, an unfamiliar muscle name often tells you most of what you need to know about it without memorizing it directly.

Part 1 — Your Turn

Apply it before we move to review

A muscle called the "extensor digitorum longus" is being described in a lab practical.

Question 1 — Based on its name alone, what movement does this muscle most likely produce?

The name directly tells you: "extensor" indicates extension, and "digitorum" refers to the digits (fingers or toes). This is a direct application of the naming-by-action convention covered this week.

Question 2 — During this extension movement, what role does this muscle play?

Since this muscle is actively producing the extension movement being described, it's acting as the agonist (prime mover) for that specific action. It would only be called the antagonist if a different muscle (like a flexor) were the one actively contracting instead.

Part 1 — Clinical Correlation

Muscle strains and the agonist/antagonist balance

This connects the functional-pair concept directly to one of the most common sports injuries.

🩺 In practice

A muscle strain occurs when a muscle is stretched or torn beyond its normal range — often when the antagonist contracts unexpectedly or too forcefully while the agonist is still lengthening. Hamstring strains are a classic example: the hamstrings (antagonist to the quadriceps during knee extension) are frequently injured during activities requiring rapid, forceful direction changes, exactly where the agonist/antagonist balance is tested hardest.

Part 2 — Ch. 12 Review

The neuron: parts and classes

Chapter 12 is the third chapter on Exam 4. Week 13 teaches neural tissue in full alongside the spinal cord; this review condenses the Ch. 12 material you need for this exam, using the same terms.

Structure: receive, integrate, conduct, transmit

  • Dendrites — branching processes that receive incoming signals.
  • Cell body (soma) — holds the nucleus and organelles; integrates incoming signals.
  • Axon hillock — the cone-shaped region where the axon leaves the cell body; action potentials typically begin at the start of the axon here (the initial segment).
  • Axon — the single long process that conducts action potentials away from the cell body; it may branch into collaterals and is often wrapped in myelin with gaps called Nodes of Ranvier.
  • Axon terminals — the axon's endings, which release neurotransmitter at a synapse.

Structural classes (by number of processes)

ClassShapeWhere you find it
MultipolarTwo or more dendrites and one axonMost common neuron in the CNS; includes the motor neurons that control skeletal muscle
UnipolarAlso called pseudounipolar; dendrites and axon are continuous, and the cell body sits off to one sideMost sensory neurons of the PNS
BipolarOne dendrite and one axon, cell body between themRare; special senses (sight, smell, hearing)
AnaxonicMany processes, no distinguishable axonBrain and special sense organs

Functional classes (by direction of signal flow)

  • Sensory (afferent) — carry signals from receptors toward the CNS.
  • Motor (efferent) — carry signals from the CNS out to effectors (muscles and glands).
  • Interneurons — connect sensory and motor neurons, mostly within the brain and spinal cord; by far the most numerous class.

Part 2 — Ch. 12 Review

Membrane potentials: resting, graded, and action

Resting membrane potential: about −70 mV. At rest, the inside of the membrane is negative relative to the outside. Three things keep it there:

  • The sodium–potassium exchange pump uses ATP to move 3 Na⁺ out for every 2 K⁺ in, keeping Na⁺ high outside the cell and K⁺ high inside.
  • At rest the membrane is far more permeable to K⁺ than to Na⁺ (leak channels), so K⁺ diffuses out and leaves the inside negative.
  • Large negatively charged proteins stay trapped inside the cell.

Three words for changes in membrane potential

  • Depolarization — the inside becomes less negative (moves toward 0 mV and beyond).
  • Repolarization — the membrane returns toward the resting potential after depolarizing.
  • Hyperpolarization — the inside becomes more negative than the resting potential.

Graded potentials vs. action potentials

FeatureGraded potentialAction potential
WhereDendrites and cell bodyAxon, starting at the initial segment
SizeVaries with stimulus strengthAll-or-none: the same size every time threshold is reached
DirectionDepolarizing or hyperpolarizingAlways depolarization, then repolarization
DistanceLocal; fades with distancePropagates the full length of the axon without weakening
SummationYes, can add togetherNo
Refractory periodNoneYes
ChannelsChemically or mechanically gatedVoltage-gated Na⁺ and K⁺ channels

Part 2 — Ch. 12 Review

The action potential, step by step

One action potential at a single patch of axon membrane. A graded depolarization must carry the membrane from −70 mV to threshold; from there the voltage-gated channels take over and the full spike follows automatically.
1
Depolarization to threshold
A graded depolarization brings the membrane to threshold, typically between −60 and −55 mV.
2
Na⁺ channels open — rapid depolarization
Voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open, Na⁺ rushes in, and the inside becomes positive, peaking at about +30 mV.
3
Na⁺ channels inactivate, K⁺ channels open — repolarization
Na⁺ channels close and inactivate while voltage-gated K⁺ channels open; K⁺ flows out and the membrane repolarizes.
4
Return to normal permeability — brief hyperpolarization
The K⁺ channels close slowly, so the membrane briefly dips below resting (toward about −90 mV) before settling back at −70 mV.

All-or-none. A stimulus that reaches threshold produces a full-size action potential; one that does not reach threshold produces none. A stronger stimulus does not make a bigger action potential — it makes them fire more often.

Refractory periods. During the absolute refractory period (from the moment Na⁺ channels open until they return to their resting state), no stimulus, however strong, can trigger another action potential. During the relative refractory period that follows (until the membrane is back at resting potential), a larger-than-normal stimulus can trigger one.

Part 2 — Ch. 12 Review

Propagation: continuous vs. saltatory

Once an action potential starts at the initial segment, it regenerates itself along the axon toward the axon terminals. It cannot travel backward, because the membrane it just left is still in its refractory period.

FeatureContinuous propagationSaltatory propagation
Axon typeUnmyelinatedMyelinated
How it movesRegenerated at each neighboring patch of membrane, step by step along the whole axon"Jumps" from one Node of Ranvier to the next; only the nodes depolarize
SpeedSlowerMuch faster
Energy costHigher: ions cross the membrane along its entire length, so the pumps work harderLower: ions cross only at the nodes

Two factors set conduction speed. Myelin (saltatory propagation is faster than continuous) and axon diameter (a larger axon offers less resistance, so the signal moves faster). The fastest axons are large-diameter and myelinated; the slowest are small-diameter and unmyelinated. This is why losing myelin, as in multiple sclerosis (covered in Week 13), slows or blocks conduction.

Part 2 — Ch. 12 Review

Signal transmission at a chemical synapse

At a chemical synapse, the presynaptic neuron's axon terminal releases a neurotransmitter that crosses the synaptic cleft and acts on the postsynaptic cell. Signals cross in one direction only. Using acetylcholine (ACh) as the example:

1
Action potential arrives
An action potential reaches the axon terminal and depolarizes it.
2
Ca²⁺ enters, neurotransmitter is released
Voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open; Ca²⁺ flows in and triggers exocytosis of synaptic vesicles, releasing ACh into the synaptic cleft.
3
Binding opens chemically gated channels
ACh diffuses across the cleft and binds receptors on the postsynaptic membrane, opening chemically gated ion channels and producing a graded potential.
4
Neurotransmitter is removed
Acetylcholinesterase (AChE) breaks ACh into acetate and choline; the presynaptic terminal reabsorbs the choline to make new ACh. Removal ends the signal so the synapse can respond again.

Three ways neurotransmitters are removed: diffusion away from the synapse, breakdown by enzymes (such as AChE), and reabsorption by the presynaptic neuron or neuroglia.

EPSP vs. IPSP. An excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP) is a graded depolarization that moves the postsynaptic membrane closer to threshold. An inhibitory postsynaptic potential (IPSP) is a graded hyperpolarization that moves it farther from threshold.

Summation. One EPSP is usually too small to reach threshold, so EPSPs must add together. Temporal summation: repeated stimuli arrive at the same synapse in rapid succession. Spatial summation: stimuli arrive at several synapses at the same time. The neuron adds all EPSPs and IPSPs together; if the net result brings the axon's initial segment to threshold, an action potential fires.

Three threads worth tracing before the exam

Thread 1 — Microscopic contraction powers macroscopic movement. Thread 2 — Naming conventions connect to joint movements from Chapter 9. Thread 3 — Neural tissue controls muscle tissue. The neuromuscular junction from Chapter 10 is a chemical synapse running Chapter 12's exact sequence: a motor neuron's action potential reaches the axon terminal, Ca²⁺ enters, acetylcholine (ACh) is released, binds receptors on the sarcolemma, and acetylcholinesterase clears it. Watch the two different calcium jobs: in the axon terminal, Ca²⁺ triggers neurotransmitter release; inside the muscle fiber, Ca²⁺ released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum exposes the myosin-binding sites on actin.

Comprehensive Practice Quiz

Exam 4 dress rehearsal — 15 questions across Ch. 10–12

Question 1
Which muscle type is voluntary and striated?
Skeletal muscle is voluntary and striated. Cardiac muscle is also striated but involuntary; smooth muscle is neither voluntary nor striated.
Question 2
A sarcomere runs from one Z-disc to the:
A sarcomere is defined as the region between two Z-discs. Fascicles and a muscle's origin are much larger structures, unrelated to the sarcomere's specific boundaries.
Question 3
Which protein makes up the thick filaments?
Myosin makes up the thick filaments. Actin makes up the thin filaments instead, and collagen is unrelated to the sarcomere's contractile proteins.
Question 4
The fixed, typically proximal attachment point of a muscle is called the:
The origin is the fixed attachment point. The insertion is the mobile end that gets pulled, and a sarcomere is a microscopic contractile unit, unrelated to whole-muscle attachment points.
Question 5
During elbow flexion, the biceps brachii acts as the:
The biceps brachii is the agonist (prime mover) for elbow flexion — it's the muscle actively producing that movement. The triceps brachii would be the antagonist in this scenario.
Question 6
A muscle named "gluteus maximus" is named according to which convention?
"Maximus" indicates size — this is the largest of the three gluteal muscles. Shape-based names (like deltoid) and origin-count names (like biceps) follow different conventions.
Question 7
Calcium's role in muscle contraction is to:
Calcium exposes the myosin-binding sites on actin, allowing contraction to begin. ATP (not calcium) provides the energy for the power stroke, and neither is involved in anchoring a muscle to bone.
Question 8
A synergist muscle's role is to:
A synergist assists the agonist or stabilizes the joint during a movement. Producing the opposite movement describes the antagonist instead, and a synergist works alongside the agonist rather than replacing it.
Question 9
"Flexor carpi radialis" is named using which combination of conventions?
"Flexor" describes the action (flexion), and "carpi radialis" describes the location (wrist, radius side) — a combination of two naming conventions, not shape/size or origin count alone.
Question 10
Rigor mortis is a direct illustration of ATP's role in muscle:
Rigor mortis shows ATP's role in relaxation — without ATP, myosin can't release actin, leaving the muscle locked in contraction. It has nothing to do with muscle growth or the naming conventions covered in Chapter 11.
Question 11
The motor neurons that control skeletal muscle are structurally classified as:
These motor neurons are multipolar — two or more dendrites and a single axon. Most sensory neurons are unipolar, and bipolar neurons are rare, found in special sense organs.
Question 12
Which neuroglial cell myelinates axons in the central nervous system?
Oligodendrocytes myelinate CNS axons. Schwann cells do the same job in the PNS, and microglia are phagocytic defense cells that make no myelin.
Question 13
During the absolute refractory period of an axon:
In the absolute refractory period the voltage-gated Na⁺ channels are already open or inactivated, so no second action potential is possible. A larger-than-normal stimulus can succeed only during the later relative refractory period.
Question 14
Saltatory propagation of an action potential:
Saltatory propagation happens in myelinated axons, where the action potential jumps between Nodes of Ranvier. It is faster and uses less energy than continuous propagation, which is the patch-by-patch pattern of unmyelinated axons.
Question 15
At a chemical synapse, what directly triggers the release of neurotransmitter from the axon terminal?
When the action potential depolarizes the axon terminal, voltage-gated channels let Ca²⁺ flow in, and that calcium triggers exocytosis of the synaptic vesicles. Binding to postsynaptic receptors happens after release, not before.

  • CNS: astrocytes, oligodendrocytes (myelin), microglia, ependymal cells
  • PNS: satellite cells, Schwann cells (myelin)

Ch. 12 — Membrane Potentials

Resting about −70 mV (Na⁺/K⁺ pump + K⁺ leak). Graded = local, variable size, can summate. Action = all-or-none, propagates, has refractory periods.

Ch. 12 — Action Potential

Threshold (about −55 mV) → Na⁺ in (depolarization, to about +30 mV) → K⁺ out (repolarization) → brief hyperpolarization → rest. Absolute refractory: no new AP. Relative: only a stronger stimulus.

Ch. 12 — Propagation

Continuous (unmyelinated, slower) vs. saltatory (myelinated, node to node, faster). Larger diameter + myelin = fastest.

Ch. 12 — Chemical Synapse

AP arrives → Ca²⁺ enters → neurotransmitter released → binds receptors → removed (diffusion, enzymes such as AChE, reabsorption). EPSP = toward threshold; IPSP = away. Summation: temporal + spatial.
