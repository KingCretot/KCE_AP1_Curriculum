Week 2 · Chapter 2

The Chemical Level of Organization

Before you can understand a cell, you need to understand the chemistry that builds it. This week moves one level below the cell — atoms, bonds, water, pH, and the four major classes of organic molecules that make up living tissue.

By the end of this week, you will be able to:

  • 1Describe the basic structure of an atom and what makes elements different from each other
  • 2Compare ionic, covalent, and hydrogen bonds
  • 3Explain why water's properties matter for the body
  • 4Use the pH scale to describe acids, bases, and the role of buffers
  • 5Identify the four major classes of organic compounds and their basic building blocks

Big Picture

Every structure in the body starts as chemistry

A muscle contracting, a nerve firing, a hormone binding to its receptor — every single one of these is, underneath the biology, a chemical event. You don't need to become a chemist this week, but you do need enough chemistry vocabulary to understand WHY the body's molecules behave the way they do.

This chapter builds the foundation for Chapter 3 (cells) directly: the cell membrane you'll study next week is literally built from the phospholipids you'll meet today, and the proteins your cells manufacture are built from the amino acids covered later in this chapter.

Structure & Function

Atomic structure: the building block of everything

Protons (positive) and neutrons (neutral) sit in the nucleus; electrons (negative) exist in a surrounding cloud. The number of protons defines the element — carbon always has 6, oxygen always has 8.

Structure & Function

Elements the body actually runs on

Only a small handful of elements make up almost the entire human body — you don't need to memorize the periodic table, just this short list.
1
Oxygen (O) — ~65% of body mass
Found in water and nearly every organic molecule.
2
Carbon (C) — ~18%
The backbone element of every organic molecule — carbs, lipids, proteins, nucleic acids.
3
Hydrogen (H) — ~10%
Found in water and bonded throughout organic molecules.
4
Nitrogen (N) — ~3%
Essential to amino acids (proteins) and nucleic acids (DNA/RNA).
5
Calcium, Phosphorus, Potassium, Sodium, and others — the remaining ~4%
Electrolytes and minerals critical for nerve signaling, bone, and fluid balance.
Memory anchor: "CHNOPS" (Carbon, Hydrogen, Nitrogen, Oxygen, Phosphorus, Sulfur) covers the elements you'll see over and over in organic molecules this semester.

Structure & Function

Ionic bonds: electrons transferred, not shared

An ion is an atom that has gained or lost electrons and now carries a charge. A cation is positively charged (lost electrons); an anion is negatively charged (gained electrons). Sodium and chloride ions — as Na+ and Cl- — are the same electrolytes your body tracks in blood tests.

Structure & Function

Covalent bonds: electrons shared

Most of the molecules that make up your body — carbohydrates, proteins, DNA — are held together primarily by covalent bonds, which are generally stronger than ionic bonds in a water-based environment like the body.

Structure & Function

Hydrogen bonds and water's special behavior

A hydrogen bond is a weak attraction between a slightly positive hydrogen on one molecule and a slightly negative region on another. Individually weak, but in huge numbers they give water its high boiling point, surface tension, and ability to dissolve so many substances — properties life depends on.

Structure & Function

Why water gets its own spotlight

Water makes up roughly 60% of total body weight, and nearly every chemical reaction in the body happens dissolved in it. Three properties matter most for this course:

  • Universal solvent — water dissolves more substances than almost any other liquid, which is why blood, cytoplasm, and interstitial fluid can carry so many dissolved nutrients, gases, and waste products at once.
  • High heat capacity — water resists rapid temperature change, which helps stabilize body temperature even when the environment fluctuates.
  • Participates in reactions — water is added or removed in many biological reactions (hydrolysis breaks bonds by adding water; dehydration synthesis builds bonds by removing water).

Structure & Function

The pH scale, acids, bases, and buffers

pH measures how acidic or basic a solution is, from 0 (most acidic) to 14 (most basic), with 7 as neutral. A buffer is a chemical system that resists pH change by absorbing excess H+ or OH- ions — this is exactly how blood keeps its pH locked in the narrow 7.35–7.45 range even when acids and bases enter the bloodstream constantly.

Structure & Function

Carbohydrates: the body's quick-energy molecules

Carbohydrates are built from repeating sugar units. Glucose (a monosaccharide) is the body's primary fuel; glycogen (a polysaccharide) is how the liver and muscles store extra glucose for later use.

Structure & Function

Lipids: energy storage and cell membranes

Triglycerides (fat storage) and phospholipids (the main membrane-building molecule) share the same glycerol-plus-fatty-acid chemistry, but phospholipids swap one fatty acid tail for a phosphate group — giving them a water-loving head and water-fearing tails, exactly the shape needed to form a membrane.

Structure & Function

Proteins: built from amino acids

There are 20 different amino acids, and the body links them in different orders and lengths to build every protein it needs — from structural proteins like collagen to functional proteins like enzymes and antibodies. The specific sequence of amino acids determines how a protein folds, and that folded shape determines its function.

Structure & Function

Nucleic acids and ATP

Nucleic acids (DNA and RNA) are built from repeating units called nucleotides. DNA stores the genetic instructions for building every protein in the body; RNA carries out those instructions during protein synthesis.

ATP (adenosine triphosphate) deserves special mention here: it's the cell's direct energy currency. Breaking the bond on its last phosphate group releases usable energy for nearly every cellular process — muscle contraction, active transport, and more. You'll see ATP again constantly starting next week.

Your Turn — Guided Practice

Apply it before the quiz

A patient's lab work shows a blood pH of 7.1 (normal range is 7.35–7.45) — a dangerously acidic reading.

Question 1 — Is a pH of 7.1 acidic, neutral, or basic relative to the normal blood range?

On the pH scale, lower numbers are more acidic. 7.1 is below the normal blood range of 7.35–7.45, making it acidic relative to normal — this condition is called acidosis.

Question 2 — What is the body system most directly responsible for resisting this kind of pH swing?

Buffers are chemical systems designed specifically to absorb excess H+ or OH- ions and resist pH change. Bone does store some minerals relevant to long-term acid-base balance, but the immediate, moment-to-moment defense against pH swings is the blood's buffer systems, not ionic bonds themselves.

Clinical Correlation

Electrolytes: ions with real clinical stakes

The ionic bonds you learned about today aren't abstract — the ions they create are checked in nearly every blood panel drawn in a hospital.

🩺 In practice

A "basic metabolic panel" blood test directly measures ions like Na⁺ (sodium), K⁺ (potassium), Cl⁻ (chloride), and Ca₂⁺ (calcium). Potassium is a good example of why this matters: both too much (hyperkalemia) and too little (hypokalemia) potassium can disrupt the electrical signals that keep the heart beating in rhythm — a life-threatening problem that starts as simple ion chemistry.

Clinical Correlation

Why IV fluids aren't just "water"

The tonicity of an IV solution — how its dissolved particle concentration compares to blood — is a direct application of this week's chemistry.

🩺 In practice

Giving a patient pure water intravenously would be dangerous — it would rush into blood cells by osmosis and cause them to swell and potentially burst. That's why IV fluids are carefully formulated (like 0.9% "normal saline") to match the body's normal solute concentration. This preview matters: osmosis and tonicity get a full, detailed treatment next week when you study the cell membrane directly.

Mini Knowledge Test

Show what you know — 7 questions

Question 1
What determines which element an atom is?
The number of protons (the atomic number) defines the element — carbon is always 6 protons, oxygen is always 8. Neutron count can vary (creating isotopes of the same element), and while atoms are usually electrically neutral (protons = electrons), it's the proton count specifically that identifies the element.
Question 2
In an ionic bond, electrons are:
Ionic bonds form when one atom fully gives up an electron and another fully accepts it, creating oppositely charged ions that attract each other. Equal sharing describes a nonpolar covalent bond instead.
Question 3
What makes water able to dissolve so many different substances?
Water's polarity (slightly charged regions) and its hydrogen bonding let it surround and separate a huge range of charged and polar substances — this is exactly why it's called the "universal solvent." Water is chemically active, not inert, and it dissolves solids and gases just as readily as other liquids.
Question 4
A blood pH of 7.2 would be classified as:
Normal blood pH is 7.35–7.45. A reading of 7.2 falls below that range, making it acidic relative to normal — even though 7.2 is technically still above the neutral point of 7.0 on the full pH scale. "Acidic" here is relative to the body's normal range, not the absolute scale midpoint.
Question 5
Which organic molecule class is built from repeating sugar units?
Carbohydrates are built from monosaccharide (single sugar) units, linked into disaccharides or polysaccharides. Lipids are built from glycerol and fatty acids; nucleic acids are built from nucleotides — different building blocks entirely.
Question 6
What makes phospholipids able to form a cell membrane?
A phospholipid's polar (water-loving) phosphate head and its nonpolar (water-fearing) fatty acid tails let it self-organize into a bilayer in a water-based environment — heads facing the water on both sides, tails hiding from it in the middle. This dual nature is exactly what triglycerides lack, since all three of their fatty acid tails are nonpolar.
Question 7
What is the direct role of ATP in the body?
ATP releases usable energy when its terminal phosphate bond breaks, making it the cell's direct energy currency for processes like muscle contraction and active transport. Storing genetic instructions is DNA's job; building membranes is primarily phospholipids' job.
