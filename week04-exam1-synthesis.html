Exam Blueprint

What's actually on Exam 1

Per the syllabus, exams in this course are closed book, closed note, built from true/false, matching, and multiple-choice questions. Exam 1 covers everything from Chapters 1–3 — nothing from Chapter 4 (Tissues) will appear.

That doesn't mean Exam 1 doesn't matter — it means a rough Exam 1 isn't the end of the world if you're rock-solid on the rest, and it's exactly why walking in well-prepared today gives you the most flexibility for the semester ahead.

The Big Picture

How Chapters 1, 2, and 3 are actually one story

It's easy to study these three chapters as separate, disconnected units. They aren't. Chapter 1 gives you the map (where things are). Chapter 2 gives you the raw material (what things are made of). Chapter 3 shows that raw material assembled into the smallest actual living unit — the cell.

Cross-Chapter Connections

Where the chapters directly touch each other

These aren't coincidences — the exam will reward you for seeing these connections, not just memorizing each chapter in isolation.

  • Ch. 2 phospholipids → Ch. 3 plasma membrane. The exact molecule you learned to draw in Week 2 — polar head, nonpolar tails — is the literal building block of the membrane in Week 3.
  • Ch. 2 amino acids → Ch. 3 membrane proteins and ribosomes. Every channel, carrier, and receptor protein in the membrane, and everything a ribosome builds, is made of the amino acids from Chapter 2.
  • Ch. 2 ATP → Ch. 3 active transport. The ATP you previewed in Week 2 is the exact energy source active transport and vesicular transport (endo-/exocytosis) spend in Week 3.
  • Ch. 1 homeostasis → Ch. 3 membrane transport. Diffusion, osmosis, and active transport are literally how the cell — the smallest unit from Chapter 1's organization hierarchy — maintains its own internal homeostasis.

Lab 2 is the hands-on partner to Chapter 3 — this is typically where you'd view actual cells under a microscope and identify the stages of mitosis in prepared slides (often onion root tip or whitefish blastula slides are the classic examples used for this).

What to reconnect before the exam: being able to identify a cell as being in prophase, metaphase, anaphase, or telophase from a microscope slide is the same skill as being able to describe what's physically happening during each of those stages from memory — the visual pattern (chromosomes condensed vs. lined up vs. separating vs. two nuclei forming) is your fastest way to tell them apart, on a slide or on an exam diagram.

Common Exam Traps

Mistakes students make most often on Exam 1

  • Mixing up medial/lateral with left/right. Medial and lateral are always relative to the body's own midline, not to the viewer's left or right.
  • Confusing negative and positive feedback direction. Remember: negative feedback opposes the change (common); positive feedback amplifies it (rare, and always ends when the triggering event finishes).
  • Mixing up ionic and covalent bonds. Ionic = electrons transferred (creates charged ions). Covalent = electrons shared (no full charge created).
  • Forgetting that "acidic" and "basic" for blood pH are relative to the normal range (7.35–7.45), not the full 0–14 scale's midpoint of 7. A pH of 7.2 is technically above neutral but still considered acidic for blood.
  • Confusing facilitated diffusion with active transport. Both use a protein, but only active transport costs ATP and only active transport can move substances against their gradient.
  • Mixing up hypertonic and hypotonic effects on cells. A simple anchor: hypotonic solutions have LESS solute outside, so water rushes IN and the cell swells — "hypo" and "big" can feel backward until this clicks.

Your Turn — Mixed Guided Practice

One question from each chapter

A quick check spanning all three chapters before the full practice set.

Question 1 (Ch. 1) — A structure is described as being closer to the point of attachment on a limb than another structure. What term describes it?

Proximal means closer to the point of attachment; distal means farther away. This is one of the pairs students flip most often under exam time pressure — a quick anchor: "proximal" and "point" both start with the sound of "p," and the point of attachment is where proximal structures are close to.

Question 2 (Ch. 2/3) — Which type of bond holds the two fatty acid tails to the glycerol backbone in a phospholipid?

The bonds linking glycerol to its fatty acid tails are covalent — shared electrons, not transferred ones. Ionic bonds create charged ions and are far less common in the stable, water-based structural molecules that make up membranes.

Comprehensive Practice

Exam 1 Full-Spectrum Quiz — 10 questions

Question 1
(Ch. 1) A transverse plane divides the body into:
Transverse = top and bottom (a cross-section). Left/right is sagittal; front/back is frontal (coronal).
Question 2
(Ch. 1) The pericardial cavity sits within the:
The pericardial cavity (surrounding the heart) is nested within the larger thoracic cavity, alongside the two pleural cavities. The abdominal cavity is below the diaphragm; the dorsal cavity holds the brain and spinal cord, an entirely separate cavity system.
Question 3
(Ch. 1) In a positive feedback loop, the response:
Positive feedback amplifies the change rather than reversing it — like labor contractions building in intensity. Reversing the stimulus is the definition of negative feedback instead.
Question 4
(Ch. 2) An atom's identity as a specific element is determined by its number of:
Protons define the element (the atomic number). Neutron count can vary within the same element (creating isotopes), and while electron count usually matches proton count in a neutral atom, it's the proton count specifically that identifies which element it is.
Question 5
(Ch. 2) A buffer's job in the blood is to:
A buffer absorbs excess H+ or OH- ions to keep pH stable within a narrow range — it doesn't push pH toward any extreme, and it isn't about ionic electron transfer at all.
Question 6
(Ch. 2) Glycogen is an example of a:
Glycogen is many linked glucose units — a polysaccharide, the body's storage form of carbohydrate. A monosaccharide is a single sugar unit (like glucose alone); a phospholipid is a lipid, an entirely different molecule class.
Question 7
(Ch. 3) Which organelle modifies and packages proteins for their final destination?
The Golgi apparatus is the "shipping department" — it modifies and packages proteins AFTER the rough ER has already built them. Mitochondria handle ATP production, an unrelated job entirely.
Question 8
(Ch. 3) Moving glucose into a cell via a specific channel protein, without using ATP, is:
A protein channel is required, but no ATP is spent — that combination specifically defines facilitated diffusion. Active transport would require ATP; simple diffusion never needs a protein channel at all.
Question 9
(Ch. 3) A cell in a hypotonic solution will:
"Hypo"tonic means LESS solute outside the cell than inside — water follows its concentration gradient INTO the cell, causing it to swell and, in extreme cases, lyse (burst).
Question 10
(Ch. 1–3 synthesis) Which sequence correctly shows how these three chapters build on each other?
Chapter 1 gives you the map and vocabulary for the body as a whole, Chapter 2 gives you the chemical raw material everything is built from, and Chapter 3 shows that raw material assembled into the smallest true living unit. They're a deliberate build, not three disconnected topics.

Final Study Sheet

Exam 1 Complete Recap — Chapters 1–3

The single condensed page to review right before you sit down for Exam 1.

Ch. 1 — Directional Terms

Superior/Inferior, Anterior/Posterior, Medial/Lateral, Proximal/Distal — always relative to anatomical position.

Ch. 1 — Planes & Cavities

Sagittal (L/R), Frontal (front/back), Transverse (top/bottom). Dorsal = Cranial + Vertebral. Ventral = Thoracic + Abdominopelvic.

Ch. 1 — Homeostasis

Negative feedback opposes change (common). Positive feedback amplifies change (rare, self-limiting).

Ch. 2 — Bonds

Ionic = transferred electrons. Covalent = shared electrons. Hydrogen = weak, but powerful in numbers (water's properties).

Ch. 2 — pH & Biomolecules

pH 0–14, 7 = neutral, blood 7.35–7.45. Carbs, lipids, proteins, nucleic acids — know their building blocks.

Ch. 3 — Membrane & Organelles

Phospholipid bilayer + proteins. Nucleus → ribosome → rough ER → Golgi = the manufacturing chain. Mitochondria = ATP.

Ch. 3 — Transport

Passive: diffusion, facilitated diffusion, osmosis (no ATP). Active: requires ATP, moves against the gradient.

Ch. 3 — Tonicity & Mitosis

Hypertonic = shrinks. Hypotonic = swells. Mitosis: Prophase → Metaphase → Anaphase → Telophase.
