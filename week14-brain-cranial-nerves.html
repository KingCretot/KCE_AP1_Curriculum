Week 14 · Chapters 14 & 15

The Brain, Cranial Nerves & Sensory-Motor Pathways

This week moves from the spinal cord upward into the brain itself (Ch. 14), then covers how sensory information travels up to the brain and motor commands travel back down (Ch. 15). Together these chapters explain how the whole nervous system — from Weeks 13's neurons and spinal cord — forms one connected communication network.

By the end of this week, you will be able to:

  • 1Identify the four major brain regions and their general function
  • 2Locate the four cerebral lobes and match each to its primary job
  • 3Describe the three meningeal layers protecting the brain
  • 4Recognize that cranial nerves emerge from the brain, not the spinal cord
  • 5Distinguish general senses from special senses
  • 6Trace a sensory signal up and a motor signal down the nervous system

Part 1 — Big Picture

Four major regions of the brain

The brain organizes into four major regions, each with a distinct job. The cerebrum — by far the largest — handles conscious thought, voluntary movement, and sensory interpretation. The cerebellum fine-tunes balance and coordination. The brainstem (midbrain, pons, medulla) runs vital, largely automatic functions like breathing and heart rate. The diencephalon (thalamus and hypothalamus) relays sensory information and regulates homeostasis.

Part 1 — Structure & Function

The cerebrum's four lobes

The cerebrum's outer surface divides into four lobes, each specializing in different functions. A simple front-to-back memory device: frontal (movement & reasoning, at the front) → parietal (touch, just behind it) → occipital (vision, at the very back) — with temporal tucked below, handling hearing and memory.

Part 1 — Structure & Function

Three protective layers: the meninges

Three membrane layers wrap the brain and spinal cord, from outside in: the tough dura mater, the web-like arachnoid mater (with cerebrospinal fluid filling the space beneath it), and the pia mater, which hugs the brain's surface directly. This same three-layer system continues down around the spinal cord.

Part 1 — Structure & Function

Cranial nerves: a different kind of nerve pair

Unlike the 31 pairs of spinal nerves covered last week, the 12 pairs of cranial nerves emerge directly from the brain and brainstem, passing through openings in the skull rather than through the vertebral column. Each is numbered with a Roman numeral (I–XII) in a fixed anterior-to-posterior order, and each serves a specific, often highly specialized function — from smell (I) to vision (II) to widespread organ control (X, the vagus nerve).

Part 1 — Clinical Correlation

Why stroke symptoms depend on location

This is a direct application of the brain-region map above.

🩺 In practice

A stroke is an interruption of blood flow to part of the brain, and its symptoms map directly onto whichever region loses blood supply. A stroke in the frontal lobe may impair movement or personality; one in the temporal lobe may affect language or memory; one in the brainstem — even though it's small — can be immediately life-threatening, since it controls breathing and heart rate. This is exactly why "time is brain": the specific deficit tells clinicians where the damage is.

Part 2 — Big Picture

Two categories of senses

Chapter 15 splits sensation into two categories. General senses (touch, temperature, pain, pressure) use simple receptors scattered throughout the body. Special senses (vision, hearing, taste, smell, balance) rely on complex, dedicated sensory organs confined to specific locations — the eye, the ear, and so on.

Part 2 — Structure & Function

The ascending (sensory) pathway

A sensory signal typically relays through a chain of neurons on its way to conscious awareness: a receptor detects the stimulus, a first-order neuron carries it into the spinal cord or brainstem, and second- and third-order neurons relay it onward — almost always through the thalamus — before it finally reaches the cerebral cortex, where it becomes a conscious sensation.

Part 2 — Structure & Function

The descending (motor) pathway

A voluntary movement follows the opposite direction: an upper motor neuron, with its cell body in the cerebral cortex, sends a command down toward the spinal cord, where it connects to a lower motor neuron whose axon exits directly to the target muscle. This two-neuron relay — and specifically where the damage occurs relative to the junction between them — is the key distinction in this week's clinical correlation.

Part 2 — Structure & Function

Four general-sense receptor types

General senses rely on four main receptor categories, each tuned to a different kind of stimulus. Recognizing which receptor type is being described — from its name alone, using the word parts above — is a fast way to answer function-based exam questions without memorizing each one separately.

Part 2 — Clinical Correlation

Upper vs. lower motor neuron damage: different signs

This distinction is one of the highest-yield concepts in this week's material, and it builds directly on the motor pathway diagram above.

🩺 In practice

Damage to an upper motor neuron (e.g., from a stroke) typically produces spastic paralysis — muscles stay tight and reflexes become exaggerated, because the lower motor neuron below the damage is still intact but has lost its usual inhibitory control. Damage to a lower motor neuron instead produces flaccid paralysis — muscles go limp and reflexes disappear entirely, because the direct connection to the muscle itself has been severed. Same symptom category (paralysis), opposite presentation, depending on which neuron is damaged.

Your Turn — Connecting Ch. 14 & 15

Apply both chapters before the quiz

A patient presents with vision loss following a head injury.

Question 1 — Which lobe of the cerebrum was most likely affected?

The occipital lobe handles vision. The frontal lobe governs movement and reasoning instead — damage there would more likely affect voluntary movement or personality, not eyesight specifically.

Question 2 — Which cranial nerve carries visual information from the eye toward the brain?

Cranial nerve II, the optic nerve, carries visual signals from the eye. Cranial nerve I (olfactory) instead carries smell — a reminder that each cranial nerve is dedicated to a specific, often single sense or function.

Practice Quiz

8 questions across Ch. 14–15

Question 1
Which brain region is primarily responsible for balance and coordination?
The cerebellum fine-tunes balance and coordination. The cerebrum handles conscious thought and movement initiation; the diencephalon relays sensory information and regulates homeostasis.
Question 2
Which cerebral lobe processes vision?
The occipital lobe, at the very back of the brain, processes vision. The parietal lobe handles touch and spatial awareness; the temporal lobe handles hearing and memory.
Question 3
Which meningeal layer is the toughest and most superficial?
The dura mater is the tough, outermost layer. The arachnoid is the web-like middle layer, and the pia mater is the innermost, directly hugging the brain's surface.
Question 4
Cranial nerves differ from spinal nerves primarily in that they:
Cranial nerves emerge from the brain and brainstem through openings in the skull. There are 12 cranial nerve pairs (not 31 — that's the spinal nerve count), and several cranial nerves carry both sensory and motor fibers.
Question 5
Vision and hearing are classified as:
Vision and hearing are special senses — they rely on complex, dedicated sensory organs (the eye, the ear) rather than the simple, widely distributed receptors of the general senses.
Question 6
In the ascending sensory pathway, signals almost always relay through the:
Nearly all sensory information relays through the thalamus before reaching the cerebral cortex and conscious awareness. The cerebellum and dura mater play no role in this specific relay.
Question 7
A nociceptor is a receptor specialized to detect:
A nociceptor detects tissue damage, producing the sensation of pain. Temperature change is detected by thermoreceptors, and light touch by mechanoreceptors instead.
Question 8
Flaccid paralysis with absent reflexes points to damage at the level of the:
Lower motor neuron damage severs the direct connection to the muscle, producing flaccid paralysis and absent reflexes. Upper motor neuron damage instead typically produces spastic paralysis with exaggerated reflexes.
