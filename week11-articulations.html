Week 11 · Chapter 9

Articulations (Joints)

Weeks 9 and 10 covered the bones themselves. This week connects them: articulations are the points where two or more bones meet, and their structure determines exactly how much — or how little — movement is possible at that point.

By the end of this week, you will be able to:

  • 1Classify joints into the three structural types
  • 2Describe the general structure of a synovial joint
  • 3Identify and demonstrate the six major joint movements
  • 4Classify synovial joints by shape and motion allowed
  • 5Connect joint structure back to the mobility/stability pattern from Chapter 8
  • 6Recognize common joint injuries and their structural basis

Big Picture

Three structural joint types

Joints are classified by how much movement they allow, which directly reflects how they're built. Fibrous joints are bound by dense connective tissue and don't move (skull sutures). Cartilaginous joints are linked by cartilage and move slightly (between vertebrae). Synovial joints have a fluid-filled cavity and move freely — this is the type covered in the most depth this week, since it's where injuries happen.

Structure & Function

Anatomy of a synovial joint

Every synovial joint shares the same basic build: two bone ends capped with slippery articular cartilage, enclosed by a joint capsule that seals in the joint (synovial) cavity, which is filled with synovial fluid — a lubricant that reduces friction and nourishes the cartilage during movement.

Structure & Function

The six major joint movements

These six terms describe nearly every movement your body makes at a joint. Flexion and extension are opposites (bending vs. straightening); abduction and adduction are opposites (away from vs. toward midline). Rotation spins around a single axis, while circumduction combines several movements into one cone-shaped sweep — think of drawing a circle with your outstretched arm.

Structure & Function

Synovial joints by shape and motion

Synovial joints are further classified by their shape, which determines exactly which movements they allow. Hinge joints (elbow, knee) move in one plane, like a door. Ball-and-socket joints (shoulder, hip) allow the widest range of motion in every direction. Pivot joints (the top of the neck) allow rotation around a single axis — the movement that lets you shake your head "no."

Your Turn — Guided Practice

Apply it before the quiz

An athlete bends their elbow to bring a weight toward their shoulder, then straightens it back out.

Question 1 — What movement occurs when the elbow bends?

Bending a joint and decreasing the angle between the two bones is the definition of flexion. Abduction refers to moving a limb away from the body's midline — a completely different type of motion, unrelated to bending.

Question 2 — Structurally, what type of joint is the elbow, based on the motion it allows?

The elbow is a hinge joint — it moves in essentially one plane, just like this scenario describes (bending and straightening only). A ball-and-socket joint like the shoulder would allow movement in many more directions than this.

Clinical Correlation

Osteoarthritis and cartilage breakdown

This directly connects the synovial joint structure covered this week to one of the most common joint diseases.

🩺 In practice

Osteoarthritis occurs when the articular cartilage capping the bone ends wears down over time, causing bone to rub directly against bone. This explains the classic symptoms — pain, stiffness, and reduced range of motion — as a direct consequence of losing the smooth, cushioned surface a healthy synovial joint depends on. It's especially common in weight-bearing joints like the knee and hip, tying back to Chapter 8's discussion of load-bearing structures.

Clinical Correlation

ACL tears and the knee's stability trade-off

This connects joint structure directly to a familiar sports injury.

🩺 In practice

The knee is a hinge joint that relies heavily on surrounding ligaments — including the ACL (anterior cruciate ligament) — for stability, since its bone shapes alone don't lock it in place the way a true hinge would. A sudden twisting force can tear the ACL, which is exactly why sports involving quick direction changes (soccer, basketball) see this injury so often — the joint's mobility comes at the cost of relying on soft tissue for stability.

Mini Knowledge Test

Show what you know — 7 questions

Question 1
Skull sutures are an example of which joint type?
Skull sutures are fibrous joints — immovable, bound by dense connective tissue. Cartilaginous joints allow slight movement, and synovial joints move freely — neither matches the skull's fixed sutures.
Question 2
The fluid that lubricates a synovial joint is called:
Synovial fluid fills the joint cavity and lubricates the joint. Cerebrospinal fluid surrounds the brain and spinal cord instead, and interstitial fluid is the general fluid between cells throughout the body — neither is specific to joints.
Question 3
Straightening a bent knee is an example of:
Extension increases the angle at a joint, which is exactly what happens when straightening a bent knee. Flexion is the opposite motion (bending), and adduction refers to movement toward the body's midline — unrelated to straightening.
Question 4
Moving your arm away from your body's midline is called:
Abduction means moving a limb away from the midline. Adduction is the opposite (toward midline), and rotation refers to spinning around an axis — a different type of motion altogether.
Question 5
Which joint type allows the widest range of motion in every direction?
Ball-and-socket joints (like the shoulder and hip) allow motion in every direction. Hinge joints move in one plane only, and pivot joints allow only rotation around a single axis — both more limited than ball-and-socket.
Question 6
Osteoarthritis primarily results from the breakdown of:
Osteoarthritis results from articular cartilage breakdown, causing bone to rub directly against bone. It's not primarily a vertebral column condition, and it doesn't directly involve skeletal muscle tissue breaking down.
Question 7
The knee relies heavily on which structures for stability, since its hinge shape alone doesn't fully stabilize it?
The knee depends on ligaments like the ACL for stability. The vertebral foramen is a spine structure unrelated to the knee, and the periosteum is the membrane covering bone — neither provides the knee's joint stability.
