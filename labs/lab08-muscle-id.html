Lab 8: Muscle Identification

Lab 8 is about finding and naming the major skeletal muscles on models and diagrams, front and back, head to ankle. You will also be expected to read a muscle's name like a set of clues. This review drills each body region with labeled diagrams, then runs a timed practical where you type the names yourself.

What this lab tests you on

Name a pinned muscle on a torso, arm, or leg model
Tell look-alikes apart (semitendinosus vs semimembranosus, teres major vs minor)
List the four rotator cuff muscles (SITS)
Name the members of the quadriceps and hamstring groups
Explain what a muscle's name says about it
Spell the names correctly

By the end of this review, you will be able to:

  • 1Decode muscle names using location, shape, size, fiber direction, number of origins, attachments, and action
  • 2Identify the superficial muscles of the head, neck, and trunk, front and back
  • 3Identify the shoulder, rotator cuff, arm, and forearm muscles
  • 4Identify the hip, thigh, and leg muscles, including every quadriceps and hamstring member
  • 5Recognize which muscles lie deep and how they show up on layered models

Your lab manual wins. Lab sections vary in which models, slides, and terms they use. If anything here differs from your lab handout or what your instructor emphasized, go with your lab material and tell your tutor so this review can be updated.

Concept Review

How muscle names work

Muscle names look intimidating until you notice they are built from a short list of clues. Most names combine two or three of them. Decode the name first and you already know roughly where the muscle sits and what it does, which is exactly what a lab practical rewards.

Clue in the nameWhat it tells youExamples
LocationThe bone or body region it lies overtemporalis (temporal bone), tibialis anterior (front of tibia), brachialis (arm), intercostals (between ribs), frontalis
ShapeWhat it looks likedeltoid (triangle), trapezius (trapezoid), rhomboid (diamond), serratus (saw-toothed), orbicularis (ring)
SizeRelative size or lengthmaximus / medius / minimus, major / minor, longus / brevis, latissimus (widest)
Fiber directionDirection the fibers run relative to the midlinerectus (straight, parallel to midline), transversus (across), oblique (on a slant)
Number of originsHow many heads the muscle hasbiceps (2), triceps (3), quadriceps (4)
AttachmentsOrigin and insertion, usually origin firststernocleidomastoid (sternum + clavicle → mastoid process), brachioradialis (arm → radius), coracobrachialis
ActionThe movement it producesflexor, extensor, adductor, levator (elevates), depressor, pronator, supinator, tensor

Try it: flexor carpi ulnaris = action (flexes) + location (carpus = wrist) + location (ulnar side). So it flexes the wrist and sits on the pinky side of the forearm. Extensor digitorum longus = extends the digits (here, the toes) and is the long one.

Spelling counts. Many practicals take points off for misspelling. The usual trouble spots: sternocleidomastoid, gastrocnemius, semimembranosus vs semitendinosus, tensor fasciae latae, rectus abdominis (not "abdominus").

Forearm groups

A
Anterior = flexors (palm side)
Lateral to medial on the superficial layer: brachioradialis (technically an elbow flexor), flexor carpi radialis, palmaris longus (thin; absent in some people), flexor carpi ulnaris. Most start at the medial epicondyle ("common flexor origin"). Flexor digitorum superficialis lies just under them. Pronator teres crosses diagonally near the elbow.
P
Posterior = extensors (back of hand side)
Lateral to medial: extensor carpi radialis longus and brevis, extensor digitorum (middle; four tendons to the fingers), extensor carpi ulnaris. Most start at the lateral epicondyle ("common extensor origin").

Orientation trick: find the thumb first. Anything with radialis is on the thumb side; anything with ulnaris is on the pinky side. Then decide palm side (flexor) or back side (extensor).

How to tell the look-alikes apart

1
Semitendinosus vs semimembranosus
Semi-T is more superficial and has a long, cord-like Tendon in its lower half. Semi-M is deeper and flatter (Membrane-like); you see it on either side of semitendinosus near the knee.
2
Which hamstring is lateral?
Only biceps femoris is lateral. It attaches to the head of the fibula. "Both semis are medial."
3
Gastrocnemius vs soleus
Gastrocnemius has two rounded heads and is superficial; soleus is flat and wider, sticking out below and beside it. Both share the calcaneal tendon.
4
Sartorius vs gracilis
Sartorius runs diagonally from the lateral hip to the medial knee. Gracilis runs straight down the medial edge of the thigh.

Naming note: newer texts say fibularis; many models and older manuals say peroneus. Both are correct. The same goes for calcaneal tendon / Achilles tendon and iliotibial tract / iliotibial band.

Check Yourself

Muscle ID quiz

Two tries per question. Your first-try score shows at the end.

Question 1
Name the muscle at the pin.
Why: The long strap crossing diagonally from the lateral hip (ASIS) to the medial knee is sartorius. Gracilis runs straight down the medial thigh; rectus femoris runs straight down the middle.
Question 2
Name the muscle at the pin.
Why: The thick muscle over the angle of the jaw, running from the zygomatic arch to the mandible, is masseter. Temporalis is the fan above the ear; buccinator is in the cheek closer to the mouth.
Question 3
Name the muscle at the pin (posterior scapula).
Why: Below the spine of the scapula = infraspinatus. Supraspinatus is above the spine, teres minor is the narrow band along the lateral border, and subscapularis is on the anterior surface.
Question 4
Which muscle is named for its shape?
Why: Deltoid means triangle-shaped (like the Greek letter delta). Brachialis is named for location, biceps for its number of heads, and adductor longus for action + size.
Question 5
The name sternocleidomastoid describes the muscle's:
Why: Sterno (sternum) + cleido (clavicle) + mastoid (mastoid process): it is named for where it attaches.
Question 6
Which muscle is not part of the rotator cuff?
Why: The rotator cuff is SITS: supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, subscapularis. Teres major sits right below teres minor but does not help stabilize the shoulder joint capsule, so it is not a cuff muscle.
Question 7
Which muscle lies deep to biceps brachii and is the prime mover of elbow flexion?
Why: Brachialis lies under the biceps and attaches to the ulna. Brachioradialis is on the lateral forearm, coracobrachialis is high on the medial arm, and triceps is on the back of the arm.
Question 8
Which hamstring is on the lateral side of the posterior thigh?
Why: Biceps femoris is the only lateral hamstring; it attaches to the head of the fibula. Both "semi" muscles are medial, and gracilis is an adductor.
Question 9
Which quadriceps muscle cannot be seen on a superficial model?
Why: Vastus intermedius lies directly deep to rectus femoris, on the front of the femur. The other three quadriceps are visible superficially.
Question 10
Whose fibers run at right angles to the external oblique?
Why: The internal oblique fibers run up and in, perpendicular to the external oblique (down and in). Transversus abdominis runs horizontally; rectus abdominis runs vertically.
Question 11
A muscle has two heads, forms the bulge of the calf, and shares the calcaneal tendon. It is the:
Why: Gastrocnemius has medial and lateral heads and is superficial. Soleus is deep to it and flat. Both insert on the calcaneus through the calcaneal tendon.
Question 12
Name the muscle at the pin (posterior thigh).
Why: This medial hamstring is superficial and has a long cord-like tendon in its lower part, the hallmark of semitendinosus. Semimembranosus lies deeper and shows on either side of it near the knee.

Watch Out

Where students lose points on this lab

The mistakeThe fix
Writing "teres major" as a rotator cuff muscleThe cuff is SITS. Teres minor is in; teres major is not.
Mixing up the two "semi" hamstringsSemiTendinosus has the long Tendon and is superficial; semiMembranosus is deeper and flatter.
Calling the lateral forearm muscle "brachialis"Brachialis is in the arm, deep to biceps. The lateral forearm muscle is brachioradialis.
Naming the wrong side on a modelLateral and medial are always relative to the model's own anatomical position. Find the thumb (radial, lateral) first.
Labeling the superficial back muscle on the deep side of the modelOn a "half-peeled" model, the side without trapezius shows rhomboids, levator scapulae, supraspinatus, and erector spinae.
Spelling: "gastrocnemus," "sternocleidomastoids," "rectus abdominus"Gastrocnemius, sternocleidomastoid (singular), rectus abdominis. Practice by typing them in the practical.
Writing only "quadriceps" when asked for a specific muscleIf a pin is on one muscle, name the muscle (e.g., vastus lateralis). Give the group name only when the question asks for the group.
Forgetting vastus intermedius existsIt is hidden under rectus femoris, but it still counts as one of the four quadriceps.
