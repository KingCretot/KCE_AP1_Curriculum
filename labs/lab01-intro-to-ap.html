Lab 1: Introduction to Anatomy & Physiology

Lab 1 is where the language of anatomy stops being a vocabulary list and becomes something you point to on a torso model, a classmate, or a diagram. Every later lab assumes you can say exactly where something is, which way a cut was made, and which cavity holds which organ. This review drills all of it until it is automatic.

What this lab tests you on

Point to a body region on a model and name it (anterior and posterior terms)
Fill in directional terms: “the wrist is ___ to the elbow”
Name the plane a specimen or image was cut in
Identify cavities and serous membranes on a torso model
Name the abdominopelvic quadrant or region for an organ or a pain location
Use the subject’s right and left, never your own

By the end of this review, you will be able to:

  • 1Describe anatomical position and use paired directional terms correctly
  • 2Name and identify the body planes and the sections they produce
  • 3Identify anterior and posterior regional terms on a body figure
  • 4Locate the dorsal and ventral cavities, their subdivisions, and their serous membranes
  • 5Map organs and symptoms to the four quadrants and the nine abdominopelvic regions

Your lab manual wins. Lab sections vary in which models, slides, and terms they use. If anything here differs from your lab handout or what your instructor emphasized, go with your lab material and tell your tutor so this review can be updated.

Concept Review · Orientation

Anatomical position and directional terms

Anatomical position: standing upright, facing forward, feet flat and slightly apart, arms at the sides, palms facing forward with thumbs pointing away from the body. Every directional term is defined from this pose, no matter how the body (or model) is actually lying. Right and left always mean the subject’s right and left. When a model faces you, its right side is on your left.

Directions on the body
Front vs. back
Term pairMeaningLab example
Superior / InferiorToward the head (cranial) / toward the feet or lower part (caudal)The nose is superior to the mouth.
Anterior (ventral) / Posterior (dorsal)Toward the front / toward the back (in humans, ventral = anterior, dorsal = posterior)The sternum is anterior to the heart; the vertebrae are posterior to it.
Medial / LateralToward the midline / away from the midline, toward the sideThe nose is medial to the eyes; the thumb is lateral to the little finger.
Proximal / DistalOn a limb: closer to / farther from where the limb attaches to the trunkThe elbow is proximal to the wrist; the toes are distal to the ankle.
Superficial / DeepToward / away from the body surfaceSkin is superficial to skeletal muscle; bone is deep to muscle.
Ipsilateral / ContralateralOn the same side / on opposite sides of the bodyThe right arm and right leg are ipsilateral; the right arm and left leg are contralateral.
IntermediateBetween a more medial and a more lateral structureThe collarbone (clavicle) is intermediate between the sternum and the shoulder.

Proximal/distal is for limbs only. Use superior/inferior on the trunk and head. “The knee is proximal to the ankle” is right; “the chest is proximal to the abdomen” is not. Some instructors also accept proximal/distal along tubes such as the GI tract or a blood vessel, so follow your lab manual.

Concept Review · Planes

Body planes and sections

A plane is an imaginary flat surface; a section is the cut you get along it. In lab you will be shown a cut brain, a model, a fruit, or an imaging slice and asked which plane made it.

Memory hook: sagittal comes from the Latin for arrow. An arrow shot at you from the front travels through the body front to back and splits left from right. Coronal follows the line of a crown or headband, running ear to ear.

Lateral view: dorsal cavity (gold) and ventral cavity (blue, tan, green)

The diaphragm separates the thoracic cavity from the abdominopelvic cavity. There is no wall between the abdominal and pelvic cavities; the dashed line from the sacrum to the pubic bone (the pelvic inlet) is the imaginary boundary.

Concept Review · Abdominopelvic Map

Four quadrants and nine regions

Two systems describe the same space. Quadrants are quick and clinical (“RLQ pain”). The nine regions are more precise and anatomical. Both use the subject’s right and left.

Quadrants: a vertical and a horizontal line crossing at the umbilicus
Nine regions: two midclavicular lines, the subcostal line, and the transtubercular line
RegionWhereMajor organs (or parts)
Right hypochondriacUpper right, under the ribsMost of the liver, gallbladder
EpigastricUpper middle, over the stomachStomach, part of the liver, pancreas, aorta
Left hypochondriacUpper left, under the ribsSpleen, part of the stomach, splenic flexure of colon
Right / left lumbarSides at waist levelAscending colon (R) / descending colon (L), parts of the kidneys and small intestine
UmbilicalCenter, around the navelSmall intestine, transverse colon
Right iliac (inguinal)Lower rightCecum and appendix
Hypogastric (pubic)Lower middleUrinary bladder, sigmoid colon, uterus (female)
Left iliac (inguinal)Lower leftDescending and sigmoid colon

Naming varies: the lumbar regions are also called lateral (abdominal) regions; the iliac regions are also called inguinal regions; the hypogastric region is also called the pubic region. All of these are accepted in the practical below. Quadrant shortcuts: RUQ = liver and gallbladder, LUQ = stomach and spleen, RLQ = appendix and cecum, LLQ = descending and sigmoid colon.

Check Yourself

Lab 1 mixed practice

Two tries per question. Read each explanation, especially when you get one right by guessing.

Question 1
In anatomical position, which way do the palms face?
Why: Palms forward is what makes the radius and ulna lie side by side and puts the thumb on the lateral side. That is why the thumb is lateral to the little finger by definition.
Question 2
A torso model faces you. The liver is on your left. Which side of the body is that?
Why: Right and left always belong to the subject. A model facing you is mirrored, so its right is on your left. The liver sits mostly in the right hypochondriac region.
Question 3
Which statement uses directional terms correctly?
Why: The wrist is farther from the shoulder than the elbow is, so it is distal. Proximal/distal is for limbs, not the trunk, and the nose is medial to the eyes.
Question 4
A brain is cut into exactly equal right and left halves. Which plane was used?
Why: Any sagittal cut separates right from left. Only a cut exactly on the midline gives equal halves: midsagittal (median). Off-center is parasagittal.
Question 5
An MRI slice shows both kidneys and both lungs, viewed as if from the front. Which plane?
Why: A frontal (coronal) plane divides front from back, so paired right and left organs appear together in the same slice.
Question 6
Which region is the back of the knee?
Why: Patellar is the front of the knee, sural is the calf, and crural is the front of the leg. The back of the knee is popliteal.
Question 7
The antecubital region is:
Why: Ante- means in front of; cubitus means elbow. The back of the elbow is olecranal, the forearm is antebrachial, and the armpit is axillary.
Question 8
Which pair are both part of the dorsal body cavity?
Why: The dorsal cavity protects the central nervous system: brain in the cranial cavity and spinal cord in the vertebral cavity. Everything else listed is ventral.
Question 9
The layer of serous membrane lying directly on the surface of a lung is the:
Why: Visceral = on the organ (viscera). Parietal = lining the wall. Pleura = lungs, pericardium = heart, peritoneum = abdominal organs.
Question 10
Which structure separates the thoracic cavity from the abdominopelvic cavity?
Why: The dome-shaped diaphragm muscle is the floor of the thoracic cavity. The pelvic inlet is the imaginary line between the abdominal and pelvic cavities.
Question 11
A patient has pain over the appendix. Which quadrant and region?
Why: The appendix hangs from the cecum in the lower right. That is the RLQ and the right iliac (inguinal) region.
Question 12
The spleen lies mainly in which region?
Why: The spleen tucks under the left ribs, posterior and lateral to the stomach: left hypochondriac region, LUQ.

Watch Out

Where students lose points on this lab

The mistakeThe fix
Using your own right and left on a model that faces you.Always name the subject’s side. Facing you = mirrored. Pause and say “its right is my left” before answering.
Using proximal/distal on the trunk (“the chest is proximal to the hips”).Proximal/distal is for limbs. Use superior/inferior for the head, neck, and trunk.
Calling any sagittal cut “midsagittal.”Midsagittal only if the halves are equal (right on the midline). Off center = parasagittal.
Mixing up frontal and transverse because both “go across.”Frontal is vertical (front/back pieces). Transverse is horizontal (top/bottom pieces).
Patellar vs. popliteal, crural vs. sural.Front of the knee and shin (patellar, crural) vs. back of the knee and calf (popliteal, sural).
Antecubital vs. antebrachial.Antecubital = front of the elbow (where blood is drawn). Antebrachial = the whole forearm.
Calling the pericardial cavity a separate division from the thoracic cavity.The pericardial cavity sits inside the mediastinum, which is part of the thoracic cavity.
Swapping visceral and parietal.Visceral touches the viscera (organ). Parietal lines the wall (think parietal bone = wall of the skull).
Labeling the nine regions from the viewer’s side.The liver is in the RIGHT hypochondriac region, which appears on your left on a figure facing you.
