Lab 2: The Cell, the Microscope, and Mitosis

Lab 2 gives you the tool you will use for the rest of the semester, the compound light microscope, and then points it at cells. You will name microscope parts and their jobs, calculate magnification, focus a slide the right way, identify cell structures on a model, and pick out each stage of mitosis on onion root tip and whitefish blastula slides.

What this lab tests you on

Point to a microscope part and name it, or state its function
Calculate total magnification for any objective
Put the focusing steps in the correct order
Identify organelles on a cell model or diagram
Name the mitotic stage of a cell under a pointer on a slide
Predict what a red blood cell does in hypo-, iso-, and hypertonic solutions

By the end of this review, you will be able to:

  • 1Identify the parts of the compound microscope and explain what each one does
  • 2Calculate total magnification and describe how field of view and depth of field change with power
  • 3Focus a specimen safely from scanning power up to high power and oil immersion
  • 4Identify the major cell structures and state each one’s function
  • 5Recognize interphase, prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase, and cytokinesis in plant and animal cells

Your lab manual wins. Lab sections vary in which models, slides, and terms they use. If anything here differs from your lab handout or what your instructor emphasized, go with your lab material and tell your tutor so this review can be updated.

Concept Review · Using the Microscope

Magnification, focusing, and what changes with power

ObjectiveOcularTotal magnificationFocus with
Scanning 4×10×40×Coarse, then fine
Low power 10×10×100×Coarse (carefully), then fine
High power 40×10×400×Fine only
Oil immersion 100×10×1000×Fine only, with immersion oil

Total magnification = ocular power × objective power. If your ocular is 15× instead of 10×, the 40× objective gives 600×.

Proper focusing procedure

1
Start low
Click the scanning (4×) objective into place. Raise the condenser and open the iris diaphragm; turn on the light.
2
Place the slide
Specimen centered over the stage opening, held by the clips or mechanical stage, coverslip up.
3
Coarse focus
Watching from the side, bring the stage up to its closest point. Then, looking through the ocular, focus with the coarse knob, moving the stage away from the lens. Sharpen with the fine knob.
4
Center, then switch up
Center the area you want; it will shrink out of view otherwise. Rotate to 10×, then 40×. Microscopes are parfocal, so the image stays nearly in focus.
5
High power rules
At 40× and 100× use only the fine knob. Add light (open the iris diaphragm) because the image gets dimmer.
6
Oil and cleanup
For 100×, swing 40× aside, place a drop of oil on the coverslip, rotate the oil objective into the drop. Afterward, clean oil off with lens paper only, return to scanning power, lower the stage, and remove the slide.
The image is inverted (upside down and backward)
As magnification goes up…What happens
Field of view (diameter of the circle you see)Decreases: you see a smaller area, larger
BrightnessDecreases: open the iris diaphragm
Depth of field (thickness in focus at once)Decreases: focus up and down with the fine knob to see layers
Working distance (lens to slide)Decreases: why the coarse knob is dangerous at high power
MovementImage moves opposite to the way you push the slide

Field-of-view math: diameter shrinks in proportion to magnification. If the field is 4.5 mm at 40×, at 400× it is 4.5 ÷ 10 = 0.45 mm (450 µm).

Cell structures and their jobs

Generalized animal cell
StructureFunctionWhat it looks like on a model
Plasma membranePhospholipid bilayer with proteins; selectively permeable boundaryOuter edge of the cell
CytoplasmEverything between the membrane and nucleus: cytosol (fluid) plus organellesThe “background” filling the cell
NucleusHolds DNA (chromatin); control centerLargest round organelle
Nuclear envelopeDouble membrane with pores around the nucleusBorder of the nucleus, with gaps (pores)
NucleolusMakes ribosomal subunits (rRNA)Dark dot inside the nucleus
RibosomesBuild proteinsTiny dots, free in cytoplasm or on rough ER
Rough ERMakes and folds proteins for export or membranesStacked, folded membranes studded with dots, next to the nucleus
Smooth ERMakes lipids and steroids; detoxifies; stores Ca2+Tubular network without dots

Rough vs. smooth ER is the classic model mix-up. Rough = dots (ribosomes) and flattened sheets right next to the nucleus. Smooth = no dots, tubes, farther out. The Golgi looks similar to rough ER but has no dots, is curved like a stack of pancakes, and sits farther from the nucleus with vesicles at its edges.

Concept Review · Osmosis

Osmosis and tonicity: the red blood cell test

Osmosis is the passive movement of water across a selectively permeable membrane toward the side with more solute (less water). Tonicity compares the solute concentration of a solution to the inside of the cell.

Red blood cells in three solutions
SolutionCompared to the cellWater movesRed blood cell resultExample
HypotonicFewer solutes outsideInto the cellSwells; may burst (hemolysis)Distilled water
IsotonicSame solute concentrationNo net movementNormal biconcave disc0.9% NaCl (normal saline)
HypertonicMore solutes outsideOut of the cellShrinks and wrinkles (crenation)Strong salt solution (e.g., 5% NaCl)

Hook: hypo sounds like “hippo”: the cell gets big. Water always chases solute.

Concept Review · Cell Cycle

Interphase, mitosis, and cytokinesis

The cell cycle is interphase (G1 growth, S DNA copying, G2 preparation) followed by the mitotic phase: mitosis (division of the nucleus) and cytokinesis (division of the cytoplasm). Most cells on any slide are in interphase because it is by far the longest stage.

StageWhat is happeningWhat you see on the slide
InterphaseCell grows; DNA replicates; not dividingIntact round nucleus, grainy chromatin, one or more dark nucleoli; no visible chromosomes
ProphaseChromatin condenses into chromosomes (sister chromatids); nucleolus and nuclear envelope break down; spindle formsThick, dark, tangled threads where the nucleus was; nucleus outline fading
MetaphaseChromosomes line up on the metaphase plate (equator), attached to spindle fibersA dark band of chromosomes across the middle of the cell
AnaphaseSister chromatids separate and move to opposite polesTwo V-shaped groups pulling apart, arms trailing toward the center
TelophaseChromosomes uncoil; new nuclear envelopes and nucleoli form at each poleTwo dark masses at opposite ends of one cell
CytokinesisCytoplasm divides (usually begins in late anaphase or telophase)Animal: cleavage furrow pinching in. Plant: cell plate forming across the middle

Concept Review · The Two Classic Slides

Onion root tip vs. whitefish blastula

FeatureOnion (Allium) root tipWhitefish blastula
Why this tissue?The root tip meristem divides constantly to grow the rootThe early embryo (blastula) divides rapidly
Cell typePlant cellAnimal cell
Cell shapeBrick-like rectangles in neat rows (files), with a cell wallRounded cells, no cell wall
Centrioles and astersNone (spindle still forms)Asters (starburst rays around the centrosomes) are easy to see
Spindle fibersFaintClearly visible lines from pole to chromosomes
CytokinesisCell plate forms in the middle and becomes a new wallCleavage furrow pinches the cell in two
Best powerScan to find the zone just behind the root cap, then 40×Find the round section on scanning, then 40×

Practical strategy: ignore the color first and look at the chromosomes. No chromosomes and an intact nucleus = interphase. Tangled = prophase. A line = metaphase. Two groups moving apart in one cell = anaphase. Two groups far apart with new nuclei, plus a plate or furrow = telophase.

Watch for this trick: two small cells side by side, each with an interphase nucleus, are two daughter cells after cytokinesis, not one cell in telophase. Look for a shared cell outline.

Check Yourself

Lab 2 mixed practice

Two tries per question. Read the explanation each time.

Question 1
Your ocular is 10× and you are using the high-power objective. Total magnification?
Why: Total = ocular × objective = 10 × 40 = 400×. The other choices are the totals at scanning (4× objective = 40×), low power (10× = 100×), and oil immersion (100× = 1000×).
Question 2
Which knob should be the only one used at 40× and 100×?
Why: Working distance is tiny at high power; the coarse knob can drive the objective into the slide. Use fine focus only.
Question 3
The image looks too dark after you switch to high power. What do you adjust?
Why: Higher power means less light reaches your eye. The iris diaphragm controls light; open it (or raise the condenser).
Question 4
You move the slide to the left. The image in the ocular moves:
Why: The compound microscope inverts the image, so movements appear reversed. That is also why a letter “e” looks upside down and backward.
Question 5
As you go from 10× to 40×, the field of view:
Why: Higher magnification shows a smaller area, enlarged. Field diameter drops by the same factor (here, four times smaller).
Question 6
Which organelle has ribosomes on its surface and lies next to the nucleus?
Why: Ribosomes make the rough ER “rough.” Smooth ER has none and makes lipids; the Golgi packages proteins after the rough ER.
Question 7
The dark spot inside the nucleus that makes ribosomal subunits is the:
Why: The nucleolus assembles ribosome subunits. It disappears in prophase and reappears in telophase.
Question 8
Chromosomes lined up across the middle of a cell indicate:
Why: Metaphase = middle. Prophase is tangled, anaphase is separating, telophase has two masses at the poles.
Question 9
Two V-shaped groups of chromosomes moving to opposite ends of one cell indicate:
Why: Sister chromatids pulled apart by spindle fibers look like V shapes, the centromere leading and the arms trailing.
Question 10
In the onion root tip, cytokinesis happens by:
Why: Plant cells have rigid walls and cannot pinch. A cell plate forms in the middle and becomes the new wall. Animal cells such as the whitefish use a cleavage furrow.
Question 11
Why are most cells on a mitosis slide in interphase?
Why: The number of cells in a stage reflects how long that stage lasts. Interphase takes up roughly 90% of the cycle.
Question 12
A red blood cell placed in distilled water will:
Why: Distilled water is hypotonic, so water moves into the cell. Crenation happens in a hypertonic solution.

Watch Out

Where students lose points on this lab

The mistakeThe fix
Using the coarse knob at 40× or 100×.Coarse only on scanning and low power. At high power, fine knob only, or you can crack the slide and scratch the lens.
Forgetting to multiply by the ocular.Total magnification = ocular × objective. The objective number alone is not the answer.
Mixing up the condenser and the iris diaphragm.Condenser = lens that focuses light. Iris diaphragm = the adjustable opening (lever) that controls how much light.
Not centering before switching to higher power.The field shrinks as power increases; center the target first or it disappears.
Calling the Golgi “rough ER” on a model.Rough ER has ribosome dots and hugs the nucleus. Golgi is a curved stack with vesicles and no dots.
Calling a prophase cell “interphase.”Interphase has a smooth, intact nucleus with a nucleolus. Visible thread-like chromosomes = prophase.
Confusing late anaphase with telophase.Anaphase: groups still moving, V shapes. Telophase: groups at the poles, clumping into new nuclei, plate or furrow visible.
Writing “cleavage furrow” for the onion.Plants form a cell plate. Cleavage furrow is for animal cells such as the whitefish blastula.
Swapping hypotonic and hypertonic.Water chases solute. Hypotonic outside = water rushes in = swelling and hemolysis. Hypertonic = crenation.
