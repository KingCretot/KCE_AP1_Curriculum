Lab 4

Lab 4: Axial Skeleton — The Skull

The skull is the most landmark-dense part of the skeleton, and in lab you will be handed one and asked to point to, or name, bones, sutures, processes, and holes from every side. This review works around the skull one view at a time — side, front, bottom, top, and the floor inside — with a label drill for each view and a timed practical at the end. Lab Exam 2 follows Labs 4–7.

What this lab tests you on

Name all 22 skull bones (8 cranial, 14 facial) on a model or diagram
Name the four major sutures: coronal, sagittal, lambdoid, squamous
Name processes and landmarks: mastoid, styloid, zygomatic arch, condyle, coronoid process
Name foramina and canals from the outside: supraorbital, infraorbital, mental, carotid canal, jugular foramen
Name the three cranial fossae and the openings in the cranial floor
Say which bone a given landmark belongs to

By the end of this review, you will be able to:

  • 1Identify the 8 cranial and 14 facial bones from lateral, anterior, inferior, and superior views
  • 2Locate the coronal, sagittal, lambdoid, and squamous sutures and the bones each one joins
  • 3Identify the major processes, fossae, and foramina of the skull and name the bone that carries each
  • 4Identify the anterior, middle, and posterior cranial fossae and the openings in the cranial floor
  • 5Tell look-alike landmarks apart and orient a skull correctly (specimen's left and right)

Your lab manual wins. Lab sections vary in which models, slides, and terms they use. If anything here differs from your lab handout or what your instructor emphasized, go with your lab material and tell your tutor so this review can be updated.

The Big Picture

22 bones: 8 cranial and 14 facial

The skull is part of the axial skeleton. Its 8 cranial bones form the vault and floor that enclose the brain; its 14 facial bones build the face, hold the teeth, and form the nasal cavity and most of the orbits. To recall the cranial bones, use "PEST OF 6": Parietal, Ethmoid, Sphenoid, Temporal, Occipital, Frontal — six names, eight bones, because the parietal and temporal bones are paired. Almost every skull bone meets its neighbors at an immovable suture; the mandible is the only one that moves freely.

Left and right always mean the specimen's. Facing a skull from the front, its right side is on your left. In an inferior (basal) view printed with the face at the top, the specimen's right is also on your left; in a superior view or cranial-floor view with the face at the top, its right is on your right. The lateral views in this lab show the skull's left side (face pointing to your left).

Sutures & Lateral Landmarks

Sutures, processes, and the mandible from the side

Turn the skull to its side and most of the practical's favorite targets appear: three of the four big sutures, the ear canal, the mastoid and styloid processes, the zygomatic arch, and every part of the mandible. The sagittal suture is the one big suture you can only see from above.

Anterior View

The orbits, the nasal cavity, and three foramina in a line

From the front, focus on two openings. Each orbit is walled by seven bones: frontal (roof), zygomatic and sphenoid (lateral wall), maxilla (floor), and lacrimal and ethmoid (medial wall), with a small piece of the palatine bone at the back of the floor. At the back of the orbit are the optic canal and the superior orbital fissure. The nasal cavity is split by the bony nasal septum (perpendicular plate of the ethmoid above, vomer below), and its side walls carry the middle concha (ethmoid) and the separate inferior nasal concha.

Inferior (Basal) View

Turn it over: the hard palate and the skull base

Flip the skull (take the mandible off first) and work from front to back: hard palate, choanae and vomer, the sphenoid with its pterygoid plates and foramen ovale, the temporal bone's mandibular fossa, styloid process, carotid canal, and jugular foramen, and finally the occipital condyles and foramen magnum. Expect several pins here, because it is the busiest view on the skull.

Cranial Floor

Inside the skull: three fossae and the openings in the floor

With the top of the skull (calvaria) removed, the floor drops in three steps from front to back, like stairs. The lesser wings of the sphenoid mark the back edge of the anterior fossa, and the petrous ridges of the temporal bones mark the back edge of the middle fossa. Many foramina you saw from below open here too, so learn them in both views.

How to Tell Them Apart

Look-alikes that cost points on the practical

Most lost points on skull practicals come from pairs of structures that sit next to each other or have similar names. Learn the one-line difference for each pair below.

Easy to confuseHow to tell them apart
Mastoid process vs. styloid processMastoid: big, round, behind the ear canal. Styloid: thin spike below and in front of the mastoid.
Mandibular condyle vs. coronoid processCondyle is posterior, rounded, and sits in the mandibular fossa. Coronoid is anterior, flat, and pointed.
Supraorbital vs. infraorbital vs. mental foramenThree holes in a vertical line: above the orbit (frontal), below the orbit (maxilla), below the premolars (mandible).
Optic canal vs. superior orbital fissureOptic canal: small round hole, medial. Superior orbital fissure: longer slit, lateral to it.
Foramen rotundum vs. ovale vs. spinosumOn each side, front to back: round, oval, then tiny ("R-O-S"). All three are in the sphenoid.
Carotid canal vs. jugular foramenFrom below, the round carotid canal is in front; the irregular jugular foramen is directly behind it.
Perpendicular plate vs. vomerBoth are in the nasal septum: perpendicular plate (ethmoid) on top, vomer below.
Middle vs. inferior nasal conchaMiddle concha is part of the ethmoid. Inferior concha is lower, larger, and a separate bone.
Crista galli vs. cribriform plateCrista galli is the vertical midline ridge; the cribriform plates are the perforated flats on either side.
External vs. internal acoustic meatusExternal: ear canal on the outside of the skull. Internal: hole on the back of the petrous part inside the skull.
Palatine process vs. palatine boneThe palatine process belongs to the maxilla (front of the palate). The palatine bone forms the back third.

Practical habit: before you name a pinned structure, say which view you are looking at and which bone it is on. Naming the bone first ("this is on the temporal bone, behind the ear canal") usually leaves only one or two possible answers.

Check Yourself

Skull mixed review

Question 1
Which of these is a cranial bone, not a facial bone?
Why: The ethmoid is one of the 8 cranial bones (PEST OF 6). Vomer, lacrimal, and palatine are facial bones.
Question 2
Which suture is seen only from above and joins the two parietal bones?
Why: The sagittal suture runs front to back in the midline of the roof. Coronal joins the frontal bone and the parietals; lambdoid joins the parietals and the occipital; squamous joins parietal and temporal.
Question 3
The zygomatic arch is formed by:
Why: Each process is named for the bone it reaches toward: the temporal bone's zygomatic process meets the zygomatic bone's temporal process.
Question 4
A pin marks the rounded knob at the back of the top of the mandibular ramus. It is the:
Why: The condyle (condylar process) is posterior and rounded and fits the mandibular fossa. The coronoid process is the flat, pointed one in front.
Question 5
Which bone forms the back third of the hard palate?
Why: The palatine processes of the maxillae make the front two-thirds; the horizontal plates of the palatine bones make the back third.
Question 6
On an inferior view, which opening lies directly in front of the jugular foramen?
Why: The round carotid canal (internal carotid artery) is immediately anterior to the irregular jugular foramen (internal jugular vein; CN IX–XI).
Question 7
The pituitary gland sits in the:
Why: The sella turcica ("Turkish saddle") is the depression on the sphenoid body in the middle of the cranial floor.
Question 8
Which cranial fossa holds the temporal lobes of the brain?
Why: Anterior = frontal lobes, middle = temporal lobes (and the pituitary in the middle), posterior = cerebellum and brainstem.
Question 9
From front to back on each side of the sphenoid, the three foramina are:
Why: Remember "R-O-S": round, oval, then the tiny spinosum just behind and lateral to the ovale.
Question 10
The superior and middle nasal conchae belong to which bone?
Why: Only the inferior nasal conchae are separate bones. The superior and middle conchae are scrolls of the ethmoid.
Question 11
You hold a skull upside down and look at its base with the face pointing away from you (at the top of your view). The specimen's right side is on:
Why: Viewed from below with the face at the top, the skull is flipped like a mirror image: its right side lies on your left. Always name sides as the specimen's.
Question 12
Which opening in the skull is shown with the pin?
Why: It is the largest opening, in the occipital bone, between the occipital condyles.

Watch Out

Where students lose points on this lab

The mistakeThe fix
Calling the styloid process the mastoid process (or the reverse).Mastoid = big, rounded, behind the ear canal. Styloid = thin spike below and in front of it.
Swapping the mandibular condyle and the coronoid process.Condyle is posterior and rounded (joint surface). Coronoid is anterior, flat, and pointed.
Calling the superior or middle nasal concha a separate bone.Only the inferior nasal concha is its own bone. The superior and middle conchae are part of the ethmoid.
Writing "palatine bone" for the whole hard palate.The front two-thirds is the palatine process of the maxilla; only the back third is palatine bone.
Mixing up the coronal and lambdoid sutures on a lateral view.Coronal is toward the face (behind the frontal bone). Lambdoid is toward the back (in front of the occipital).
Mixing up foramen ovale, rotundum, and spinosum.Front to back: rotundum, ovale, spinosum ("R-O-S"); spinosum is the tiniest.
Confusing the external and internal acoustic meatus.External = ear canal on the outside. Internal = hole on the back face of the petrous part, inside the skull.
Naming sides from your own point of view.Left and right are always the specimen's. Facing the skull, its right side is on your left.
Misspelling on the practical (ethmoid, lambdoid, sella turcica, crista galli).Write the names out by hand a few times before lab. Many practicals take points off for spelling.
