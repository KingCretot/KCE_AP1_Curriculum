Lab 9: Muscle Actions

Lab 9 takes the muscles you identified in Lab 8 and asks what each one does. You will demonstrate movements on yourself, name the prime mover and its antagonist, and read a muscle's action from where it attaches. This review teaches the roles, the joint-by-joint action tables, and the antagonistic pairs, then lets you practice on pull-line diagrams and in a timed practical.

What this lab tests you on

Define origin, insertion, agonist (prime mover), antagonist, synergist, and fixator
Demonstrate a movement and name the prime mover
Name the antagonist of a given muscle
State the action of a pinned muscle on a model
Use the correct movement term (flexion, abduction, dorsiflexion, and so on)
Explain what two-joint muscles do at each joint

By the end of this review, you will be able to:

  • 1Explain how a muscle moves a bone by pulling its insertion toward its origin
  • 2Assign agonist, antagonist, synergist, and fixator roles for a described movement
  • 3Name the prime movers for the main movements of the shoulder, elbow, wrist, hip, knee, ankle, trunk, jaw, and face
  • 4Pair each major muscle with its antagonist
  • 5Predict a muscle's action from its position relative to a joint

Your lab manual wins. Lab sections vary in which models, slides, and terms they use. If anything here differs from your lab handout or what your instructor emphasized, go with your lab material and tell your tutor so this review can be updated.

Concept Review

Origin, insertion, and the four muscle roles

Lab 8 asked where a muscle is. Lab 9 asks what it does. Every answer comes from one rule: a muscle can only pull, and when it shortens it pulls its insertion toward its origin. If you know where the two ends attach and which joint lies between them, you can work out the action.

TermMeaningElbow example (lifting a cup)
OriginThe attachment that stays fixed during the movement; usually proximalBiceps brachii: scapula (supraglenoid tubercle and coracoid process)
InsertionThe attachment that moves; usually distalBiceps brachii: radial tuberosity
ActionThe movement produced when the muscle contractsFlexes the elbow; supinates the forearm
Agonist (prime mover)The muscle most responsible for the movementBrachialis and biceps brachii
AntagonistOpposes the agonist; relaxes and stretches during the movement, then reverses itTriceps brachii
SynergistAssists the prime mover or cancels an unwanted motionBrachioradialis
FixatorA synergist that holds the origin bone still so the prime mover has a stable baseRotator cuff and scapular muscles steadying the scapula
Right elbow, lateral view. Biceps (red arrow) pulls the radius toward the shoulder; triceps (blue) pulls the olecranon the other way.

Roles depend on the movement. No muscle is permanently an agonist. When you flex the elbow, biceps is the agonist and triceps is the antagonist. When you extend it (pushing up from a chair), the roles swap. On a practical, always ask: "Which movement are we talking about?"

Naming note: some courses call biceps brachii the prime mover of elbow flexion; others give that title to brachialis, because it flexes the elbow in every forearm position. Accept either, and know that both are prime movers in most texts, with brachioradialis as the synergist.

💭 Use the joint position rule. A muscle that lies on the anterior side of the shoulder, elbow, wrist, or hip usually flexes it; a muscle on the posterior side usually extends it. The knee and ankle are the exceptions you must memorize: the anterior thigh (quadriceps) extends the knee, the posterior thigh (hamstrings) flexes it, and the anterior leg dorsiflexes while the calf plantar flexes.

Two-joint muscles. Rectus femoris crosses the hip and the knee, so it flexes the hip and extends the knee. The hamstrings cross both too: they extend the hip and flex the knee. Gastrocnemius crosses the knee and ankle: it plantar flexes the ankle and helps flex the knee. Soleus crosses only the ankle, so it is a pure plantar flexor. Expect at least one practical question on this.

How to answer a "demonstrate and name" station

1
Do the movement yourself
Move your own joint the way the prompt describes. Feel which side of the joint tightens.
2
Name the movement precisely
Joint + movement term: "flexion of the elbow," "abduction of the arm at the shoulder," "plantar flexion at the ankle."
3
Find the muscle that crosses that side of the joint
Use the joint position rule (anterior flexes, posterior extends; reversed at knee and ankle). Name the prime mover first.
4
Name the antagonist
It lies on the opposite side of the same joint. If asked for a synergist, give the helper listed in the action tables.

Check Yourself

Muscle actions quiz

Two tries per question. Red marks the muscle that is contracting.

Question 1
The red muscle contracts. What happens at the elbow?
Why: Biceps brachii lies on the anterior arm and inserts on the radial tuberosity, so it pulls the forearm up toward the shoulder: flexion. It also supinates the forearm.
Question 2
The red muscle contracts. What happens at the elbow?
Why: Triceps brachii lies on the posterior arm and inserts on the olecranon. Pulling the olecranon up straightens the elbow: extension.
Question 3
The red muscle is the prime mover of which movement?
Why: Deltoid caps the shoulder and inserts on the deltoid tuberosity on the lateral humerus. Its middle fibers lift the arm out to the side.
Question 4
The red muscle contracts. What happens at the knee?
Why: Rectus femoris is a quadriceps muscle. Through the patella and patellar ligament it pulls the tibia forward, extending the knee. Because it also crosses the hip, it flexes the hip too.
Question 5
The red muscle contracts. What happens at the ankle?
Why: Tibialis anterior runs down the front of the leg to the medial foot. Pulling the top of the foot toward the shin is dorsiflexion (it also inverts the foot).
Question 6
The red muscles contract. What happens at the ankle?
Why: Gastrocnemius and soleus pull the calcaneus up through the calcaneal tendon, pointing the toes down: plantar flexion (standing on tiptoe).
Question 7
During elbow flexion, the triceps brachii is the:
Why: The triceps opposes flexion; it relaxes and lengthens so the flexors can work. In elbow extension it becomes the agonist.
Question 8
Which muscle is the antagonist of the quadriceps femoris at the knee?
Why: Quadriceps extend the knee; the hamstrings flex it. Gastrocnemius assists knee flexion but the hamstrings are the prime movers.
Question 9
Rectus femoris flexes the hip and extends the knee because it:
Why: It arises from the anterior inferior iliac spine (above the hip) and inserts through the patellar ligament on the tibial tuberosity (below the knee). The other three quadriceps arise from the femur, so they act only at the knee.
Question 10
A synergist is a muscle that:
Why: Synergists help the prime mover or cancel unwanted motion. The opposite movement is the antagonist's job.
Question 11
Which muscle is the prime mover of hip flexion?
Why: Iliopsoas (iliacus + psoas major) crosses the front of the hip to the lesser trochanter. Gluteus maximus is its antagonist (hip extension); gluteus medius abducts.
Question 12
You turn your face to the left. Which sternocleidomastoid contracted?
Why: One SCM turns the face to the opposite side. Both together flex the neck.

Watch Out

Where students lose points on this lab

The mistakeThe fix
Saying the quadriceps "flex" the knee because they are anteriorThe knee is the exception: anterior thigh extends the knee, posterior thigh flexes it.
Writing "flexion" when the station asks about the ankleAt the ankle use dorsiflexion (toes up) and plantar flexion (toes down).
Calling a muscle "the antagonist" without naming the movementRoles change with the movement. Biceps is the agonist for elbow flexion and the antagonist for elbow extension.
Thinking muscles pushMuscles only pull: insertion moves toward origin. The opposite movement needs a muscle on the other side of the joint.
Giving only one action for a two-joint muscleRectus femoris: hip flexion + knee extension. Hamstrings: hip extension + knee flexion. Gastrocnemius: plantar flexion + knee flexion.
Turning the head the wrong way for SCMOne SCM turns the face to the opposite side.
Mixing up supination and pronationSupination = palm up, "holding a bowl of soup." Pronation = palm down.
Listing deltoid as an adductorDeltoid abducts. Pectoralis major and latissimus dorsi adduct.
