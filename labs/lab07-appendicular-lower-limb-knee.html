Lab 7

Lab 7: Appendicular Skeleton — The Lower Limb & Knee Joint

This lab finishes the appendicular skeleton: the pelvic girdle, every bone of the lower limb, and the knee joint. On models and loose bones you will name bones and markings, tell a right bone from a left one, sex a pelvis, and point to the menisci and ligaments of the knee. Lab Exam 2 follows Labs 4–7.

What this lab tests you on

Name the three parts of the hip bone and its landmarks (crest, spines, notches, tuberosity, rami, acetabulum, obturator foramen)
Tell a female pelvis from a male pelvis
Name femur, patella, tibia, and fibula landmarks on any view
Name all 7 tarsals, the metatarsals, and the phalanges
Decide whether a loose lower-limb bone is from the right or left side
Identify the menisci, cruciate and collateral ligaments, patellar ligament, and bursae of the knee

By the end of this review, you will be able to:

  • 1Identify the ilium, ischium, and pubis and every major landmark of the hip bone in lateral and anterior views
  • 2Compare the female and male pelvis using the subpubic angle, inlet shape, and overall build
  • 3Identify the landmarks of the femur, patella, tibia, and fibula and use them to side each bone
  • 4Identify the tarsals, metatarsals, and phalanges of the foot
  • 5Describe the knee joint: its bones, joint type, menisci, ligaments, bursae, and movements

Your lab manual wins. Lab sections vary in which models, slides, and terms they use. If anything here differs from your lab handout or what your instructor emphasized, go with your lab material and tell your tutor so this review can be updated.

Pelvic Girdle

The hip bone (os coxae): three bones fused into one

The pelvic girdle is the two hip bones (also called os coxae or coxal bones). Each starts as three bones — ilium, ischium, and pubis — that fuse by adulthood, and all three meet in the acetabulum, the socket for the femoral head. The hip bones join the sacrum posteriorly at the sacroiliac joints and each other anteriorly at the pubic symphysis. Together with the sacrum and coccyx they form the bony pelvis.

Spine vs. notch, top to bottom along the back edge: PSIS → PIIS → greater sciatic notch → ischial spine → lesser sciatic notch → ischial tuberosity. Say it out loud while running a finger down the posterior border of a model.

Pelvic Girdle

Female vs. male pelvis

On the practical you may be handed a pelvis and asked its sex. Start with the subpubic angle (the angle under the pubic symphysis): make an L with your thumb and index finger — if the arch is wider than your L, it is likely female. Then check the inlet shape and overall heaviness. The female pelvis is built wider and shallower for childbirth.

True vs. false pelvis: the pelvic brim (from the sacral promontory along the arcuate lines to the top of the pubic symphysis) divides the false (greater) pelvis above, between the iliac blades, from the true (lesser) pelvis below. The opening at the brim is the pelvic inlet; the opening at the bottom, framed by the coccyx, ischial tuberosities, and pubic arch, is the pelvic outlet.

Thigh & Leg

Femur, patella, tibia, and fibula

The femur is the longest and strongest bone in the body. The patella is a sesamoid bone inside the quadriceps tendon. In the leg, the tibia is the medial, weight-bearing bone; the thin fibula runs along its lateral side, joined to it by the interosseous membrane. Learn which landmarks are anterior and which are posterior — that is how you orient a loose bone.

Foot

Tarsals, metatarsals, and phalanges

The foot has 26 bones: 7 tarsals, 5 metatarsals, and 14 phalanges. Mnemonic for the tarsals from posterior to anterior and medial to lateral: "Tiger Cubs Need MILk" — talus, calcaneus, navicular, medial / intermediate / lateral cuneiforms; the cuboid sits lateral to the cuneiforms, in front of the calcaneus.

Left or Right?

Siding loose bones

Lab practicals often hand you a loose bone and ask "right or left?" Right and left always mean the specimen's own right and left, not yours. The method is always the same: put the bone in anatomical position (proximal end up, anterior surface facing you), find one feature whose medial/lateral position you know, and read the side.

BoneOrient it like thisThen read the side
Hip boneAcetabulum facing laterally (toward you), iliac crest upThe pubis and ASIS point anteriorly. If anterior is on your right, it is a right hip bone
FemurHead up, patellar surface (smooth groove) facing youThe head points medially. Head on your left = left femur; head on your right = right femur
PatellaApex down, articular (posterior) surface facing youThe larger lateral facet is the side cue. Larger facet on your right = right patella
TibiaPlateau up, tibial tuberosity facing youThe medial malleolus is medial. Malleolus on your right = right tibia
FibulaHead up; the flat, triangular articular facet on the lateral malleolus faces medially (toward the tibia), and the malleolar fossa (pit) just behind it faces posteriorlyView it from behind (fossa toward you): facet pointing to your left = right fibula; facet pointing to your right = left fibula
Foot / tarsalsToes away from you, dorsal surface upThe big toe and navicular are medial; the cuboid and fifth metatarsal are lateral. Big toe on your left = right foot
Left femur, anterior view: head on your left
Left tibia and fibula, anterior view: medial malleolus on your left

Quick self-check: stand in anatomical position and hold the bone against your own leg on the side you think it came from. If the head of the femur points toward your midline and the patellar surface faces forward, you guessed right.

Knee Joint

The knee: bones, menisci, ligaments, and bursae

The knee is the largest and most complex synovial joint in the body. Functionally it is a hinge joint (some textbooks say "modified hinge"): the main movements are flexion and extension, with slight medial and lateral rotation possible only when the knee is flexed. At full extension the femur rotates slightly medially on the tibia, "locking" the knee so you can stand with little muscle effort; the popliteus muscle "unlocks" it to begin flexion.

Cruciate memory trick: each cruciate is named for where it attaches on the tibia. They cross like an X: the ACL runs to the lateral femoral condyle, the PCL to the medial one. ACL stops the tibia moving Anteriorly; PCL stops it moving Posteriorly.

Clinical link: a hard blow to the lateral side of a planted knee can tear the tibial collateral ligament, the medial meniscus (fused to it), and the ACL together — the "unhappy triad." ACL tears are among the most common sports injuries.

Check Yourself

Lower limb and knee review

Question 1
Which three bones fuse to form each hip bone?
Why: The sacrum and coccyx are axial bones. The ilium, ischium, and pubis fuse at the acetabulum.
Question 2
The "sit bone" you feel when you sit on a hard chair is the:
Why: The ischial tuberosity is the roughened lowest part of the ischium and bears your weight when you sit.
Question 3
A pelvis has a subpubic angle of about 70°, a heart-shaped inlet, and heavy, rough markings. It is most likely:
Why: A narrow (less than 90°) pubic arch and a heart-shaped inlet are classic male features.
Question 4
This hip bone is in lateral view. Which side is it from?
Why: In lateral view the acetabulum faces you. The pubis and the anterior superior iliac spine point anteriorly — here, to your right — so it is a right hip bone.
Question 5
Which femoral landmark is on the POSTERIOR surface?
Why: The linea aspera is the long rough ridge down the back of the shaft. The intertrochanteric line and patellar surface are anterior.
Question 6
This femur is shown in anterior view. Which side is it?
Why: The head always points medially. With the patellar surface facing you, the head is on your left, so this is a left femur.
Question 7
The patellar ligament runs from the patella to the:
Why: The quadriceps tendon becomes the patellar ligament below the patella and inserts on the tibial tuberosity.
Question 8
Which bone is NOT part of the knee joint?
Why: The fibula only reaches the underside of the lateral tibial condyle (proximal tibiofibular joint) and gives attachment to the fibular collateral ligament.
Question 9
Which tarsal bone articulates with the tibia and fibula to form the ankle joint?
Why: The trochlea of the talus fits between the medial and lateral malleoli. The calcaneus is the heel below it.
Question 10
A soccer player's tibia is pushed forward relative to the femur and the knee becomes unstable. Which ligament is most likely torn?
Why: The ACL prevents anterior displacement of the tibia on the femur (and limits hyperextension).
Question 11
Which meniscus is C-shaped and fused to the tibial collateral ligament, making it the one torn more often?
Why: The medial meniscus is anchored to the tibial (medial) collateral ligament, so it cannot slide out of the way. The lateral meniscus is O-shaped and more mobile.
Question 12
Kneeling for long periods irritates the bursa between the skin and the patella. Which bursa is it?
Why: The prepatellar bursa lies just under the skin over the patella; inflammation is sometimes called "housemaid's knee."

Watch Out

Where students lose points on this lab

The mistakeThe fix
Siding a bone from your own point of view.Right and left are always the specimen's. Put the bone in anatomical position first, then find a medial feature (femoral head, medial malleolus, big toe).
Calling the ischial spine the ischial tuberosity.Spine = small, pointed, higher, between the two sciatic notches. Tuberosity = big rough "sit bone" at the bottom.
Mixing up the greater and lesser sciatic notches.Greater is the big, deep notch on the ilium above the ischial spine; lesser is the small notch below the spine.
Swapping the intertrochanteric line and crest.Line = anterior and faint; crest = posterior and raised. If the linea aspera is facing you, you are looking at the crest side.
Listing the fibula as part of the knee joint.The knee is femur + tibia + patella. The fibula only anchors the fibular collateral ligament.
Reversing the cruciate attachments.Named by tibial attachment: ACL starts anterior on the tibia and runs to the lateral femoral condyle; PCL starts posterior and runs to the medial condyle.
Calling the medial meniscus the lateral one on a superior view of the tibia.Medial is C-shaped and larger; lateral is nearly a closed O. On a right tibia seen from above with anterior at the top, medial is on your left.
Confusing the navicular (foot) with the scaphoid (hand).Both are boat-shaped, but navicular = foot, between the talus and the cuneiforms. Scaphoid = wrist.
