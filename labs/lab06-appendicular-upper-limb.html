Lab 6: Appendicular Skeleton — The Upper Limb

This lab is all about the bones of the shoulder, arm, forearm, wrist, and hand. On real bones and models you will name each bone, point to its landmarks, and decide whether a loose bone came from the left or the right side. This review teaches each bone with a "where it is / how to spot it" table, drills every landmark with numbered pins, and finishes with a timed practical. Lab Exam 2 follows Labs 4–7, so this material carries forward.

What this lab tests you on

Name the bones of the pectoral girdle: clavicle and scapula
Point to scapular landmarks: spine, acromion, coracoid process, glenoid cavity, fossae, borders, angles
Name humerus landmarks: head, necks, tubercles, sulcus, deltoid tuberosity, capitulum, trochlea, epicondyles, fossae
Tell the radius from the ulna and name their landmarks (olecranon, notches, heads, styloid processes)
Name all 8 carpals and identify metacarpals and phalanges
Decide whether a bone is from the left or right side

By the end of this review, you will be able to:

  • 1Identify the clavicle and every major landmark of the scapula from anterior, posterior, and lateral views
  • 2Identify the landmarks of the humerus and say what each one articulates with or anchors
  • 3Distinguish the radius from the ulna and identify the landmarks of each
  • 4Name the carpals in order, number the metacarpals, and name the phalanges
  • 5Use a two-step method (find the view, then point a landmark) to tell left bones from right bones

Your lab manual wins. Lab sections vary in which models, slides, and terms they use. If anything here differs from your lab handout or what your instructor emphasized, go with your lab material and tell your tutor so this review can be updated.

Pectoral Girdle

The clavicle and the scapula

Each pectoral girdle is two bones: the clavicle (collarbone) in front and the scapula (shoulder blade) behind. The clavicle is the girdle's only bony link to the axial skeleton (at the sternoclavicular joint); the scapula is held to the trunk mostly by muscle. That loose attachment buys the shoulder its huge range of motion. Throughout this lab, "left" and "right" always mean the specimen's left and right, as if the bone were still in a body standing in anatomical position — not your left and right as you look at it.

Arm

The humerus: proximal end, shaft, and distal end

The humerus is the only bone of the arm (brachium). Learn it in three zones. The proximal end has the head, two necks, two tubercles, and the groove between them. The shaft has the deltoid tuberosity laterally and the radial groove on the back. The distal end carries the two articular surfaces (capitulum laterally, trochlea medially), the two epicondyles, and three fossae that make room for forearm bones when the elbow bends and straightens.

Match the partners: capitulum ↔ head of the radius; trochlea ↔ trochlear notch of the ulna; coronoid fossa ↔ coronoid process (elbow flexed); olecranon fossa ↔ olecranon (elbow extended); radial fossa ↔ head of the radius (elbow flexed). If you can name the partner, you can usually name the landmark.

Forearm

The radius and the ulna — and how to tell them apart

The forearm (antebrachium) has two bones side by side. In anatomical position (palms forward) the radius is lateral, on the thumb side, and the ulna is medial, on the little-finger side. They are joined along their shafts by the interosseous membrane and meet each other at the proximal and distal radioulnar joints.

Wrist & Hand

Carpals, metacarpals, and phalanges

The hand skeleton has 27 bones: 8 carpals (wrist), 5 metacarpals (palm), and 14 phalanges (fingers). Read the carpals in two rows, always from lateral (thumb side) to medial. A common mnemonic: "Some Lovers Try Positions That They Can't Handle" — scaphoid, lunate, triquetrum, pisiform (proximal row), then trapezium, trapezoid, capitate, hamate (distal row). Trapezium goes with the thumb.

Right hand, palmar view (thumb on the viewer's left)
GroupBonesWhere / how to identify
Carpals (8), proximal rowScaphoid, lunate, triquetrum, pisiform (lateral → medial)Scaphoid = boat-shaped, on the thumb side, meets the radius; lunate = moon-shaped, next to it; triquetrum = pyramid-shaped; pisiform = small pea on the palmar surface of the triquetrum
Carpals, distal rowTrapezium, trapezoid, capitate, hamate (lateral → medial)Trapezium sits under the thumb metacarpal; trapezoid is small and wedge-shaped; capitate is the largest, central bone; hamate has a palmar hook
Metacarpals (5)Numbered I–V from the thumbLong bones of the palm; each has a base (proximal), shaft, and head (the knuckle)
Phalanges (14)Proximal, middle, distalFingers II–V have three each; the thumb (pollex) has only proximal and distal

Model tips: the scaphoid and lunate are the two carpals that meet the radius. The pisiform and the hook of the hamate are both on the palmar side, so if you can see them you are looking at the palm. Metacarpals are numbered I–V starting from the thumb; the knuckles you make with a fist are the metacarpal heads. The singular of phalanges is phalanx; "pollex" is the thumb.

Left or Right?

Telling a left bone from a right bone

Left and right are always the specimen's sides, judged as if the bone were in a body in anatomical position. Use the same two steps every time: (1) find the view — which surface is anterior and which is superior — then (2) point one landmark to the side it belongs on (the humeral head and glenoid cavity point toward or away from the midline; the thumb and radius are lateral). The safest check on a real bone is to hold it against your own body in anatomical position and see which side it fits.

Watch the trap: a posterior view of a right humerus and an anterior view of a left humerus both have the head on the viewer's left. The view decides the answer — so always find an anterior marker (intertubercular sulcus, coronoid fossa, capitulum) or a posterior marker (deep olecranon fossa, radial groove) first.

Check Yourself

Upper limb quiz

Two tries per question. The explanation appears after you answer.

Question 1
Which end of the clavicle articulates with the manubrium of the sternum?
Why: The rounded sternal end forms the sternoclavicular joint. The flat acromial end meets the acromion of the scapula.
Question 2
A pin sits in the large depression on the posterior scapula below the spine. What is it?
Why: Below the spine = infraspinous; above = supraspinous. The subscapular fossa is the whole anterior surface.
Question 3
Which scapular process hooks anteriorly from the superior border, just lateral to the suprascapular notch?
Why: The coracoid process points forward. The acromion is the flattened lateral end of the spine at the top of the shoulder.
Question 4
The capitulum of the humerus articulates with the:
Why: Capitulum (lateral, round) ↔ radial head. The trochlea (medial spool) meets the trochlear notch of the ulna.
Question 5
Which fossa on the posterior distal humerus receives the olecranon when the elbow is extended?
Why: The deep olecranon fossa is posterior. The coronoid and radial fossae are anterior and fill when the elbow flexes.
Question 6
A common humerus fracture site lies just distal to the greater and lesser tubercles. It is the:
Why: The surgical neck is the narrowing below the tubercles. The anatomical neck is the rim right around the head.
Question 7
Which forearm bone has its "head" at the distal (wrist) end?
Why: The ulna's head is distal; the radius's head is the disc at the proximal end.
Question 8
On which bone would you find the ulnar notch?
Why: The ulnar notch is on the medial side of the distal radius; the head of the ulna sits in it. (The radial notch is on the ulna.)
Question 9
Which carpal articulates with the base of the thumb's metacarpal?
Why: Trapezium goes with the thumb — the saddle-shaped carpometacarpal joint of the thumb.
Question 10
Which carpal is pea-shaped and sits on the palmar surface of the triquetrum?
Why: Pisiform means pea-shaped. It is the most medial bone of the proximal row.
Question 11
This humerus is shown in anterior view (intertubercular sulcus and coronoid fossa visible). Which side is it from?
Why: In anterior view the head points medially. Here the head is on the viewer's left, so the medial side is on the viewer's left and the bone is a left humerus. It cannot be a posterior view: the sulcus and coronoid fossa are anterior landmarks (and a left humerus seen from behind would have its head on the viewer's right).
Question 12
This scapula is shown in posterior view (the spine faces you). Which side is it from?
Why: The spine tells you it is posterior. The glenoid cavity points laterally, and here it is on the viewer's left. Viewed from behind, a left scapula's lateral side is on your left, so it is a left scapula.

Watch Out

Where students lose points on this lab

The mistakeThe fix
Swapping the acromion and the coracoid process.Acromion = the lateral end of the spine, on top of the shoulder (meets the clavicle). Coracoid = the hook pointing anteriorly; you see it best from the front or side.
Mixing up the coronoid process (ulna) and the coronoid fossa (humerus).A process sticks out; a fossa is a dent. The ulna's coronoid process drops into the humerus's coronoid fossa when the elbow bends.
Swapping capitulum and trochlea.Capitulum is lateral and round (meets the radius). Trochlea is medial and spool-shaped (meets the ulna).
Swapping radial notch and ulnar notch.Each notch is named for the bone that fits into it: the radial notch is on the ulna; the ulnar notch is on the radius.
Calling the radial head "the head of the ulna" (or the reverse).Radius head = proximal disc at the elbow. Ulna head = small distal knob at the wrist.
Writing "anatomical neck" for the surgical neck.Anatomical neck hugs the head. Surgical neck is lower, below both tubercles.
Mixing up trapezium and trapezoid.Trapezium = thumb side, under metacarpal I. Trapezoid is the small wedge next to it, under metacarpal II.
Calling left/right from your own point of view.Use the specimen's side. Find the view first (anterior or posterior), then point a landmark (humeral head and glenoid face medially/laterally; thumb and radius are lateral).
Misspelling on the practical (olecranon, capitulum, triquetrum, glenoid, acromion).Write the names out by hand a few times. Many practicals deduct for spelling.
