Lab Exam 2 Review: The Skeleton & Joint Movements

Lab Exam 2 asks you to do two things with your hands: name bones and bone markings on real models, and name (or perform) the movement a joint is making. This review walks the whole skeleton head to toe, then the joints, with a label drill for every region and a timed practical at the end. Note: Labs 4–7, which likely feed this exam, will be added once your tutor has the lab schedule — use this review for the skeleton and joint content now.

What this lab tests you on

Name any bone of the skull, spine, thoracic cage, girdles, and limbs on a model
Name bone markings: sutures, processes, foramina, fossae, condyles, tuberosities
Identify a vertebra's region (cervical, thoracic, lumbar) and C1/C2 by their features
Tell left from right and male from female on loose bones and pelves
Classify joints structurally and functionally; name synovial joint types with examples
Name movements: flexion, abduction, rotation, pronation, dorsiflexion, inversion, and more

  • 1Identify the 22 skull bones and the key skull features and sutures from lateral, anterior, superior, and inferior views

  • 3Identify every bone and major marking of the pectoral girdle, upper limb, pelvic girdle, and lower limb
  • 4Classify joints by structure and function and match each synovial joint type to an example
  • 5Name every movement on the course list from a picture or a demonstration

Big Picture

Axial vs. appendicular, and the 22 bones of the skull

The adult skeleton has 206 bones. The axial skeleton (80) is the skull, hyoid, auditory ossicles, vertebral column, and thoracic cage. The appendicular skeleton (126) is the pectoral and pelvic girdles plus the limbs. The skull alone has 22 bones: 8 cranial and 14 facial. A good way to remember the cranial bones: "PEST OF 6" — Parietal, Ethmoid, Sphenoid, Temporal, Occipital, Frontal (6 different bones, 8 total because parietal and temporal are paired).

Skull Features

Sutures, processes, and openings you will be asked to point to

Most skull bones meet at immovable sutures (fibrous joints). On the practical, expect pins on sutures and on the processes and openings below. Look at the skull from more than one side: the palatine bones, vomer, foramen magnum, and occipital condyles are easiest from below; the sagittal suture is only seen from above.

Vertebral Column

Five regions, one basic plan — and the features that give each region away

Count the regions top to bottom: 7 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar (breakfast at 7, lunch at 12, dinner at 5), then the sacrum (5 fused) and coccyx (3–5 fused). Every typical vertebra has a body in front, a vertebral arch behind (two pedicles + two laminae), a vertebral foramen for the spinal cord, one spinous process, two transverse processes, and superior and inferior articular processes. Adult curvatures: cervical and lumbar curve forward (concave posteriorly), thoracic and sacral curve backward.

Thoracic Cage & Upper Limb

Sternum, ribs, pectoral girdle, and upper limb

Sternum: manubrium (with the jugular notch and clavicular notches) → sternal angle (where rib 2 attaches; a landmark for counting ribs) → body → xiphoid process. Ribs (12 pairs): true ribs 1–7 attach to the sternum by their own costal cartilage; false ribs 8–12 do not — ribs 8–10 attach to the cartilage above (vertebrochondral), and 11–12 float (no anterior attachment). Some courses list "false ribs 8–10" and "floating 11–12" as separate groups — both conventions are fine; just know floating ribs are technically false ribs too.

Pelvic Girdle & Lower Limb

Hip bones, the male vs. female pelvis, and the lower limb

Each hip bone (os coxae) forms from three bones that fuse at the acetabulum: the ilium (top, wing-shaped), ischium (bottom back; you sit on the ischial tuberosities), and pubis (front). The two hip bones meet anteriorly at the pubic symphysis and posteriorly at the sacrum (sacroiliac joints). The bony pelvis = both hip bones + sacrum + coccyx.

Joints

Joint classification and the six synovial joint types

Joints are classified two ways. Structurally by what binds the bones (fibrous, cartilaginous, synovial) and functionally by how much they move: synarthrosis (immovable), amphiarthrosis (slightly movable), diarthrosis (freely movable).

Structural classWhat holds the bones togetherExamplesUsual functional class
FibrousDense connective tissue, no cavitySutures (skull), syndesmosis (distal tibiofibular, interosseous membrane), gomphosis (tooth in socket)Synarthrosis (immovable) — syndesmoses are slightly movable
CartilaginousCartilage, no cavitySynchondrosis (epiphyseal plate, first rib–sternum, hyaline); symphysis (pubic symphysis, intervertebral discs, fibrocartilage)Amphiarthrosis (slightly movable) — synchondroses are immovable
SynovialFibrous capsule around a fluid-filled joint cavityShoulder, elbow, hip, knee, wrist, knuckles, TMJDiarthrosis (freely movable)
A typical synovial joint (sectioned)

Every synovial joint has articular cartilage (hyaline) on the bone ends, a joint (synovial) cavity filled with synovial fluid, a two-layer articular capsule (outer fibrous capsule, inner synovial membrane that makes the fluid), and reinforcing ligaments. Many also have bursae, tendon sheaths, or menisci (knee).

Synovial joint types (by shape)

Plane (gliding)
Intercarpal, intertarsal, acromioclavicular. Nonaxial gliding.
Hinge
Elbow (humeroulnar), knee, interphalangeal. Uniaxial: flexion/extension.
Pivot
Atlantoaxial (C1–C2), proximal radioulnar. Uniaxial: rotation.
Condylar (ellipsoid)
Wrist (radiocarpal), metacarpophalangeal (knuckles). Biaxial.
Saddle
Carpometacarpal joint of the thumb. Biaxial — makes opposition possible.
Ball-and-socket
Shoulder (glenohumeral), hip. Multiaxial: every movement incl. rotation.

Axes shortcut: plane = nonaxial; hinge and pivot = uniaxial; condylar and saddle = biaxial; ball-and-socket = multiaxial. Some textbooks call the condylar joint "ellipsoid" or "condyloid" — same joint.

Movements

Every movement on the list, in pictures

Movement names describe what happens from anatomical position. Most come in opposite pairs. In each panel the gray dashed limb is the starting position and the red arrow shows the motion. On the practical you may be shown a picture, given a description, or asked to perform the movement — practice doing each one with your own body while saying its name.

Flexion
Decreases the angle between bones (bending the elbow).
Extension
Increases the angle; returns to anatomical position.
Hyperextension
Extension past anatomical position (tipping the head back).
Abduction
Moves a limb away from the midline.
Adduction
Moves a limb toward the midline.
Rotation
Turns a bone around its own long axis (shaking the head "no").
Circumduction
Distal end traces a circle; the limb sweeps a cone.
Supination
Palm turns anterior (up); radius and ulna parallel.
Pronation
Palm turns posterior (down); radius crosses over ulna.
Dorsiflexion
Toes lift toward the shin (heel-walking).
Plantar flexion
Toes point down (standing on tiptoe).
Inversion
Sole turns medially (inward).
Eversion
Sole turns laterally (outward).
Elevation
Lifts a part superiorly (shrugging the shoulders).
Depression
Lowers a part inferiorly (dropping the shoulders, opening the jaw).
Protraction
Moves a part anteriorly in the horizontal plane (jutting the jaw).
Retraction
Moves a part posteriorly (pulling the jaw back).
Opposition

PairTip for keeping them straight
Flexion / extensionFlexion decreases the joint angle (bending); extension increases it. Hyperextension goes past anatomical position.
Abduction / adductionAb- = away from the midline ("abducted" = taken away); ad- = add back to the body. Fingers and toes use the middle finger / second toe as the reference line.
Pronation / supinationSupination = palm up, "holding a bowl of soup"; radius and ulna parallel. Pronation crosses the radius over the ulna.
Dorsiflexion / plantar flexionDorsi- = top of the foot moves toward the shin. Plantar = toward the sole (pointing the toes).
Inversion / eversionInversion turns the sole IN (medially) — the classic ankle-sprain direction.
Elevation / depression; protraction / retractionUp/down vs. forward/backward. Both pairs apply to the scapulae and the mandible.
Rotation vs. circumductionRotation turns a bone on its own long axis; circumduction makes the distal end trace a circle (a cone of motion).
OppositionThumb touches the other fingertips — possible because the thumb's carpometacarpal joint is a saddle joint.

Check Yourself

Lab Exam 2 mixed review

Question 1
A vertebra has a hole in each transverse process and a short, forked spinous process. Which region?
Why: Transverse foramina are found only in cervical vertebrae, and a bifid spinous process is typical of C2–C6.
Question 2
Which bone has NO body and no spinous process?
Why: The atlas is a ring of two arches with large lateral masses. The axis is the one with the dens.
Question 3
The bump you feel just behind your earlobe is the:
Why: The mastoid process is a thick, rounded projection behind the ear canal. The styloid is the thin spike below the ear and cannot be felt through the skin.
Question 4
Which suture joins the two parietal bones?
Why: Sagittal runs front to back in the midline. Coronal joins frontal and parietals; lambdoid joins parietals and occipital; squamous joins parietal and temporal.
Question 5
On a loose humerus, the rounded knob lateral to the trochlea that articulates with the radius is the:
Why: Capitulum = "little head," lateral, meets the radial head. The trochlea is the spool that fits the ulna's trochlear notch.
Question 6
Which carpal bone articulates with the base of the thumb's metacarpal?
Why: Trapezium: "the Thumb sits on the Trapezium." The trapezoid sits next to it under metacarpal II.
Question 7
A pelvis has a subpubic angle of about 110° and a wide oval inlet. It is most likely:
Why: A wide (over 90°) pubic arch and an oval inlet are classic female features adapted for childbirth.
Question 8
The tibial tuberosity is the attachment for the patellar ligament. Where is it?
Why: It is the bump you feel below your kneecap on the front of the tibia.
Question 9
Standing on tiptoe is which movement at the ankle?
Why: Plantar flexion points the toes toward the sole. Dorsiflexion lifts the toes toward the shin.
Question 10
Which movement is shown?
Why: The radius (gold) crosses over the ulna and the back of the hand faces forward — pronation.
Question 11
The pubic symphysis is structurally a ____ joint and functionally an ____.
Why: A pad of fibrocartilage joins the pubic bones and allows slight movement.
Question 12
Which synovial joint type allows rotation only, like the proximal radioulnar joint?
Why: A pivot joint is uniaxial around a long axis: atlantoaxial joint and proximal radioulnar joint.

Watch Out

Where students lose points on this lab

The mistakeThe fix
Calling the styloid process the mastoid process (or the reverse).Mastoid = big, rounded, behind the ear canal. Styloid = thin spike below and in front of it.
Mixing up the coronoid process (ulna and mandible) with the coronoid fossa (humerus).A process sticks out; a fossa is a dent. The ulna's coronoid process fits into the humerus's coronoid fossa when the elbow flexes.
Swapping capitulum and trochlea.Capitulum = lateral, round, meets the radius. Trochlea = medial spool, meets the ulna.
Swapping radius and ulna.In anatomical position the radius is on the thumb side. The ulna has the big olecranon at the elbow; the radius has the big end at the wrist.
Naming a lumbar vertebra "thoracic" because it is big.Look for costal facets. No facets + huge body + hatchet spinous process = lumbar.
Calling the whole hip bone "the pelvis" or "the ilium."One side = hip bone (os coxae) made of ilium, ischium, and pubis. The pelvis = both hip bones + sacrum + coccyx.
Mixing up inversion/eversion and supination/pronation.Inversion and eversion are at the foot (sole turns in or out). Supination and pronation are at the forearm (palm turns up or down).
Writing "rotation" for circumduction.Rotation spins a bone on its own axis. Circumduction draws a circle with the distal end (a cone).
Misspelling on the practical (ethmoid, acetabulum, olecranon, calcaneus).Write the names out by hand a few times before the exam. Most practicals take points off for spelling.
