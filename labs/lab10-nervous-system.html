Lab 10 & Lab Exam 3 Review: Brain, Cranial Nerves, Reflexes & Autonomic Responses

This is your final lab review, and the final lab exam draws on everything from Chapters 12 through 16: neural tissue, the spinal cord and reflexes, the brain and cranial nerves, and the autonomic nervous system. Expect to name structures on brain models and spinal cord diagrams, match cranial nerves to their tests, describe reflex results, and predict autonomic effects. Work through the diagrams until you can name every pin without the word bank.

What this lab tests you on

Brain surface: lobes, gyri, sulci, brainstem, cerebellum
Midsagittal structures and the ventricles
Cranial nerves I–XII: number, name, type, test
Spinal cord cross section and the reflex arc
Reflex tests and what a normal response looks like
Sympathetic vs parasympathetic effects on organs

By the end of this review, you will be able to:

  • 1Identify the lobes, major sulci and gyri, brainstem parts, and cerebellum on a lateral view
  • 2Identify diencephalon, commissures, ventricles, and meninges on midsagittal and coronal views
  • 3Name, number, classify, and test all twelve cranial nerves
  • 4Label a spinal cord cross section and trace the five parts of a reflex arc
  • 5Describe normal responses for the patellar, Achilles, biceps, triceps, plantar, and pupillary reflexes, and predict sympathetic vs parasympathetic effects

Your lab manual wins. Lab sections vary in which models, slides, and terms they use. If anything here differs from your lab handout or what your instructor emphasized, go with your lab material and tell your tutor so this review can be updated.

Concept Review · Brain Surface

The brain from the side: lobes, gyri, and sulci

On the lab practical you will be handed a brain model (or a preserved sheep or human brain) and asked to point to or name surface landmarks. Start with the big picture: the brain has four major regions, and every structure you name belongs to one of them.

Concept Review · Ventricles & Meninges

CSF, the ventricles, and the meninges

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) fills four ventricles inside the brain and the subarachnoid space around it. Lab models show the ventricles as a blue cast; the meninges are tested on a coronal section model or diagram.

Ventricles projected onto a lateral view (anterior is to the left)
1
Make
Choroid plexuses (capillaries plus ependymal cells) in each ventricle produce CSF.
2
Flow
Lateral ventricles → interventricular foramen → third ventricle → cerebral aqueduct → fourth ventricle → out through the median and lateral apertures into the subarachnoid space (a little goes down the central canal).
3
Drain
Arachnoid granulations push into the superior sagittal sinus and return CSF to venous blood.
Coronal section through the top of the head showing the meninges
Layer or space (outside to inside)What to look forNotes
Dura materThick, tough outer membrane just under the skullTwo layers in the skull (periosteal and meningeal); they split to form dural sinuses. Folds: falx cerebri, tentorium cerebelli, falx cerebelli
Subdural spacePotential space between dura and arachnoidSite of a subdural hematoma
Arachnoid materThin, web-like middle membraneDoes not dip into sulci; granulations poke into the superior sagittal sinus
Subarachnoid spaceSpace under the arachnoid with web-like strandsFilled with CSF and contains the large blood vessels; the needle target for a lumbar puncture
Pia materDelicate layer clinging to every gyrus and sulcusCannot be peeled off the brain surface
Epidural spaceFat-filled space outside the duraReal space only around the spinal cord (where epidural anesthesia goes); in the skull the dura is fused to bone

Memory trick, outside in: D-A-P — "Don’t Annoy the Patient": dura, arachnoid, pia. "Mater" means mother: the dura is the tough mother, the pia is the gentle mother.

Concept Review · Cranial Nerves

The twelve cranial nerves

Twelve pairs of cranial nerves leave the brain (not the spinal cord). They are numbered I–XII from front to back in the order they emerge. On the lab practical you may have to point to a nerve on an inferior brain model, give its number and name, say whether it is sensory, motor, or both, or name the nerve tested by a simple bedside test.

Inferior (ventral) view: cranial nerves I–XII on one side; anterior is at the top

Names mnemonic: "Oh Once One Takes The Anatomy Final, Very Good Vacations Are Heavenly" (Olfactory, Optic, Oculomotor, Trochlear, Trigeminal, Abducens, Facial, Vestibulocochlear, Glossopharyngeal, Vagus, Accessory, Hypoglossal).

Type mnemonic (S = sensory, M = motor, B = both): "Some Say Marry Money, But My Brother Says Big Brains Matter More."

Naming differences you may see: vestibulocochlear is sometimes called the auditory or acoustic nerve (older terms); accessory is also called the spinal accessory nerve; "both" is also written as "mixed." Some texts list III, IV, VI, XI, and XII as "mainly motor" because they also carry muscle-sense (proprioceptive) fibers, and some count III, VII, IX, and X as carrying parasympathetic fibers. Use the labels your lab manual uses.

Concept Review · Spinal Cord & Reflex Arc

Spinal cord cross section and the reflex arc

The spinal cord in cross section is an H (or butterfly) of gray matter wrapped in white matter, the opposite of the cerebrum. Models are usually drawn with the posterior (dorsal) side up, so find the narrow posterior median sulcus (top) and the wider anterior median fissure (bottom) first; they tell you which way is which.

Spinal cord cross section; posterior (dorsal) is at the top; roots are drawn on one side
StructureContainsTip
Posterior (dorsal) hornInterneurons that receive sensory inputThin horns reaching toward the back
Anterior (ventral) hornCell bodies of somatic motor neuronsWide, blunt horns toward the front
Lateral hornCell bodies of autonomic (sympathetic) motor neuronsSmall side bump, present in the thoracic and upper lumbar cord (T1–L2)
Gray commissure and central canalCrossing fibers; canal holds CSFThe crossbar of the H with the canal in the middle
White columns (funiculi): posterior, lateral, anteriorMyelinated tracts running up (sensory) and down (motor)Posterior funiculi are also called the dorsal columns
Dorsal (posterior) rootSensory (afferent) axons onlyEnters the posterior side of the cord
Dorsal root ganglionCell bodies of sensory neuronsSwelling on the dorsal root
Ventral (anterior) rootMotor (efferent) axons onlyLeaves the anterior side; no ganglion
Spinal nerveBoth sensory and motor axons (mixed)Formed where the two roots join; 31 pairs
Reflex arc for the patellar (knee-jerk) stretch reflex
1
Receptor
Detects the stimulus. In the patellar reflex, the muscle spindle senses the quadriceps being stretched by the tap.
2
Sensory (afferent) neuron
Carries the signal through the spinal nerve and dorsal root; its cell body sits in the dorsal root ganglion.
3
Integration center
The synapse(s) in the CNS gray matter. A stretch reflex is monosynaptic (sensory neuron synapses directly on the motor neuron); most other reflexes, like withdrawal, add interneurons (polysynaptic).
4
Motor (efferent) neuron
Cell body in the anterior horn; its axon leaves through the ventral root and spinal nerve.
5
Effector
The muscle or gland that responds. Here the quadriceps femoris contracts and the knee extends.

Concept Review · Reflex Tests

Testing reflexes in lab

In lab you test reflexes on a partner and record whether each response is normal, weak, absent, or exaggerated. Know how each test is done, what a normal response looks like, and which spinal cord levels (or cranial nerves) it checks.

Six reflex tests: where to tap or stimulate and the normal response
ReflexHow to test itNormal responseChecks
Patellar (knee-jerk)Subject sits with legs dangling; tap the patellar ligament just below the kneecapQuadriceps contracts; knee extends (leg kicks forward)L2–L4 (femoral nerve)
Achilles (ankle-jerk, calcaneal)Foot slightly dorsiflexed; tap the calcaneal tendon above the heelGastrocnemius and soleus contract; foot plantar flexesS1–S2 (tibial nerve)
BicepsElbow bent about 90°; press your thumb on the biceps tendon at the elbow crease and tap your thumbBiceps contracts; elbow flexes slightlyC5–C6 (musculocutaneous nerve)
TricepsArm supported, elbow bent about 90°; tap the triceps tendon just above the olecranonTriceps contracts; elbow extendsC6–C7 (radial nerve)
PlantarFirmly stroke the lateral sole from heel to the ball of the foot, then across toward the big toeToes curl downward (flexor or "negative Babinski" response)L5–S1 and the corticospinal (upper motor neuron) pathway
Pupillary lightDim the room; shine a penlight into one eyeBoth pupils constrict: direct (lit eye) and consensual (other eye)CN II in, CN III out (parasympathetic); midbrain
Plantar response: normal adult versus Babinski sign

Reading the results: reflexes are graded 0 (absent) to 4+ (very brisk, with clonus); 2+ is normal. If a partner’s reflex is weak, use reinforcement (the Jendrassik maneuver: hook the fingers together and pull) to boost it. Absent reflexes with limp muscles point to a lower motor neuron problem; exaggerated reflexes and a Babinski sign in an adult point to an upper motor neuron problem. Two cranial reflexes also show up on lab sheets: the corneal (blink; V in, VII out) and the gag (IX in, X out).

Naming: the patellar reflex is also called the knee-jerk or quadriceps reflex, and the Achilles reflex the ankle-jerk or calcaneal tendon reflex. Some manuals list the triceps reflex as C7–C8 and the plantar reflex as L4–S2; use your manual’s levels.

Concept Review · Autonomic Nervous System

Sympathetic vs parasympathetic responses

The autonomic nervous system (ANS) runs the organs without conscious effort. It always uses two motor neurons in a row: a preganglionic neuron from the CNS to a ganglion, then a postganglionic neuron to the organ. In lab you will predict which division takes over in a scenario and what each organ does.

Sympathetic versus parasympathetic outflow and effects on the same organs
FeatureSympathetic ("fight or flight")Parasympathetic ("rest and digest")
Origin in CNSThoracolumbar: lateral horns of T1–L2Craniosacral: brainstem (CN III, VII, IX, X) and S2–S4
GangliaClose to the cord (sympathetic chain, collateral ganglia)Close to or in the organ (terminal or intramural ganglia)
Fiber lengthsShort preganglionic, long postganglionicLong preganglionic, short postganglionic
Neurotransmitter at the organMostly norepinephrine (adrenergic)Acetylcholine (cholinergic)
Both divisionsPreganglionic neurons release acetylcholinePreganglionic neurons release acetylcholine
OrganSympathetic effectParasympathetic effect
HeartRate and force increaseRate decreases (vagus nerve)
PupilsDilateConstrict (oculomotor nerve)
Airways (bronchioles)DilateConstrict
Digestion (stomach and intestines)Slows: less motility and secretion; sphincters closeSpeeds up: more motility and secretion; sphincters relax
Urinary bladderWall relaxes, internal sphincter closes (hold urine)Wall contracts, sphincter relaxes (urination)
Salivary glandsSmall amount of thick saliva (dry mouth)Lots of watery saliva
Blood vesselsMost constrict (skin and digestive organs), so blood pressure rises; vessels in skeletal muscle dilateLittle or no effect on most vessels
Sweat glands, adrenal medulla, liverSweating; epinephrine release; glucose released into bloodNo effect

Scenario shortcut: ask "Is this body running, scared, or exercising, or is it resting after a meal?" Exam stress, a near-miss in traffic, a sprint, or a scary movie = sympathetic. Lying on the couch after Thanksgiving dinner, needing to urinate, a watering mouth, or a slow resting pulse = parasympathetic.

Check Yourself

Lab Exam 3 check: 12 questions

Pick the best answer. Two questions show a model with a structure marked in red.

Question 1
A lab partner points to the ridge directly in front of the central sulcus. What is it, and what does it do?
Why: Pre = before (anterior). The precentral gyrus, in the frontal lobe, sends voluntary motor commands. The postcentral gyrus, behind the sulcus in the parietal lobe, receives body sensation.
Question 2
Name the structure marked in red on this midsagittal section.
Why: The thalamus is the large oval mass in the center of the diencephalon, forming the walls of the third ventricle. The hypothalamus sits below and in front of it.
Question 3
CSF leaves the third ventricle through which passage?
Why: The path is lateral ventricles → interventricular foramen → third ventricle → cerebral aqueduct → fourth ventricle. Arachnoid granulations return CSF to the superior sagittal sinus.
Question 4
Name the nerve marked in red on this inferior view.
Why: The trigeminal is the thick nerve leaving the side of the pons. The abducens leaves at the junction of the pons and medulla near the midline; VII and VIII leave just behind the pons, laterally.
Question 5
Which meningeal layer clings to every gyrus and dips into every sulcus?
Why: The pia mater is the delicate innermost layer fused to the brain surface. The arachnoid bridges over the sulci; the dura is the tough outer layer, and the falx cerebri is a fold of dura.
Question 6
A patient cannot shrug the right shoulder or turn the head against resistance. Which cranial nerve is involved?
Why: The accessory nerve supplies the trapezius and sternocleidomastoid. The hypoglossal moves the tongue; the vagus works the pharynx, larynx, and organs; the facial runs facial expression.
Question 7
In the spinal cord, cell bodies of somatic motor neurons are found in the:
Why: Anterior horn = somatic motor cell bodies. Posterior horn = sensory interneurons. Dorsal root ganglion = sensory cell bodies. Lateral horn (T1–L2) = sympathetic motor cell bodies.
Question 8
Which reflex arc component is the quadriceps femoris in the patellar reflex?
Why: The effector is the muscle or gland that responds. The receptor is the muscle spindle, the integration center is the synapse in the cord gray matter, and the motor neuron carries the command out.
Question 9
Tapping the calcaneal tendon tests which spinal cord segments?
Why: Achilles (ankle-jerk) = S1–S2. Patellar = L2–L4. Biceps = C5–C6. Triceps = C6–C7 (some manuals say C7–C8).
Question 10
Stroking the sole of a healthy adult’s foot should produce:
Why: Toes curling down is the normal plantar (flexor) response. Big toe up and toes fanned is the Babinski sign: normal in infants under about one year, but a sign of corticospinal (upper motor neuron) damage in adults.
Question 11
Light is shone into the left eye only. Normally:
Why: The pupillary light reflex has a direct response (lit eye) and a consensual response (other eye). CN II carries the signal in; CN III carries parasympathetic output to both irises.
Question 12
A student is about to give a speech: heart racing, dry mouth, pupils wide. Which statement is true?
Why: Fight-or-flight: heart rate and force up, pupils dilate, airways dilate, saliva scant and thick, digestion slows, and the bladder wall relaxes so urine is held.

Watch Out

Where students lose points on this lab

The mistakeThe fix
Mixing up precentral and postcentral gyrusFind the central sulcus first. The gyrus in front (frontal lobe) is motor; the gyrus behind (parietal lobe) is sensory. Motor comes first, like "pre" does.
Calling the hypothalamus the thalamus (or the reverse)"Hypo" = below. The thalamus is the big central oval; the hypothalamus is the small region under and in front of it, with the pituitary hanging from it.
Pointing to the fornix and calling it the corpus callosumThe corpus callosum is the thick upper arch. The fornix is the thinner arch beneath it, with the septum pellucidum stretched between them.
Confusing the cerebral aqueduct with the central canalThe aqueduct runs through the midbrain (third to fourth ventricle). The central canal runs down the spinal cord from the fourth ventricle.
Reading the spinal cord upside downLocate the wide anterior median fissure and the thin posterior median sulcus. Posterior horns are slender; anterior horns are broad. The ganglion is always on the dorsal root.
Writing "VI" when the question asks for a name (or the reverse)Read the prompt. When it says "name," write abducens; when it says "number," write VI. Writing both (abducens, VI) is safest if your instructor allows it.
Misspelling on a typed or written practicalWatch the usual traps: vestibulocochlear, glossopharyngeal, trochlear, abducens, mammillary, arachnoid, pellucidum, oblongata.
Calling the Babinski sign a normal adult responseBig toe up is normal only in infants. In an adult, the normal plantar response is toes curling down.
Thinking the parasympathetic division raises anythingParasympathetic = rest and digest: it slows the heart, constricts pupils and airways, and speeds digestion, salivation, and urination. Sympathetic does the reverse and also drives sweating and constricts most blood vessels.
