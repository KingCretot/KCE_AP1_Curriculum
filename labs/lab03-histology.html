Lab 3: Histology — Reading Tissue Slides

Histology lab is a picture test. Your instructor puts a slide under a microscope (or a photo on a screen) and you name the tissue, point out a feature, or say where in the body it lives. This review teaches you what each tissue actually looks like in a stained field of view, so you can recognize it instead of guessing.

What this lab tests you on

Name the tissue on a slide or photo (epithelium = layers + cell shape)
Identify a feature: cilia, goblet cells, lacunae, intercalated discs, central canal
Give one place the tissue is found in the body
Give the tissue's main function
Tell look-alikes apart (dense regular vs. smooth muscle, hyaline vs. elastic cartilage)
Name the four primary tissue types

By the end of this review, you will be able to:

  • 1Name all seven epithelia by number of layers and the shape of the surface cells
  • 2Identify the ten connective tissues by their matrix, fibers, and cells
  • 3Tell skeletal, cardiac, and smooth muscle apart by striations, nucleus position, and branching
  • 4Find the neuron cell body, nucleus, nucleolus, dendrites, axon, and neuroglia on a nervous tissue slide
  • 5Match each tissue to a body location and its main job

Your lab manual wins. Lab sections vary in which models, slides, and terms they use. If anything here differs from your lab handout or what your instructor emphasized, go with your lab material and tell your tutor so this review can be updated.

Epithelial Tissue

Epithelium: name it by layers + surface cell shape

The two-word rule. First word = layers: simple (one layer), stratified (two or more), or pseudostratified (looks layered, isn't). Second word = the shape of the cells at the free (apical) surface: squamous (flat), cuboidal (as tall as wide), columnar (taller than wide). Stratified tissues are always named for the top layer, even though their basal cells are cuboidal.

How to find epithelium on a slide: look for an edge. Epithelium always has one free surface facing a lumen, a cavity, or the outside, and it sits on a basement membrane with connective tissue underneath. It has no blood vessels of its own (avascular).

Connective Tissue

Connective tissue: read the matrix

Connective tissue is mostly matrix. Epithelium is cells packed shoulder to shoulder; connective tissue is cells scattered in an extracellular matrix of ground substance plus fibers (collagen, elastic, reticular). Ask three questions: Is the matrix fluid, gel, rubbery, or hard? Which fibers do I see, and are they parallel or random? What are the cells sitting in? Your course groups these into five families: loose (areolar, adipose, reticular), dense (regular, irregular), cartilage, bone, and blood.

Cells to know: fibroblasts make fibers in connective tissue proper; chondrocytes live in lacunae in cartilage; osteocytes live in lacunae in bone; adipocytes store fat; blood has erythrocytes, leukocytes, and platelets. Cartilage has no blood vessels, so it heals slowly; bone is richly vascular.

Muscle & Nervous Tissue

Muscle and nervous tissue: striations, nuclei, branching

The muscle three-question test. (1) Striations? No → smooth. Yes → go on. (2) Where are the nuclei? Many at the edge → skeletal. One in the middle → cardiac. (3) Confirm cardiac with branching and intercalated discs, the dark cross lines where cells lock together with gap junctions and desmosomes.

Neuron parts: the cell body (soma) holds the nucleus, a prominent nucleolus, and dark-staining chromatophilic (Nissl) bodies (rough ER). Dendrites are several short, tapering branches that receive signals. The single axon leaves from a pale, cone-shaped axon hillock and carries signals away. The many small dark dots around the neuron are nuclei of neuroglia (glial cells), which support and protect neurons.

Slide-ID Strategy

How to identify any slide in under a minute

1
Scan at low power
Find the overall pattern before zooming. Is there a free edge with open space on one side? Packed cells along an edge = epithelium. Mostly matrix with scattered cells = connective. Long parallel fibers = muscle. Huge star-shaped cells among tiny dots = nervous.
2
Epithelium
Count layers from the basement membrane up (one, many, or "fake layers" with nuclei at different heights). Name the surface cell shape. Check extras: cilia, goblet cells, keratin, dome cells.
3
Connective
Fluid with loose cells = blood. Lacunae in a glassy matrix = cartilage (no fibers = hyaline; dark fibers = elastic; pink bundles in rows = fibrocartilage). Rings around canals = bone. Fibers only: parallel = dense regular, random = dense irregular, loose web = areolar, black mesh = reticular. Big empty circles = adipose.
4
Muscle
Striations, nucleus position, branching, intercalated discs (see the three-question test).

Dense regular CT vs. smooth muscleDense regular: wavy pink bundles with flat, thin nuclei squeezed between bundles. Smooth muscle: cells with tapered ends and plump cigar nuclei inside each cell.
Skeletal vs. cardiac muscleBoth striated. Skeletal: unbranched, many peripheral nuclei. Cardiac: branched, central nucleus, intercalated discs.
Simple columnar vs. pseudostratifiedSimple columnar: nuclei in one tidy row; microvilli (fuzzy line). Pseudostratified: nuclei scattered at many heights; cilia (distinct hairs).
Stratified squamous vs. transitionalStratified squamous: surface cells flat. Transitional: surface cells large and dome-shaped, sometimes with two nuclei.
Hyaline cartilage vs. boneBoth have lacunae. Bone: concentric rings around central canals, canaliculi. Hyaline: smooth glassy matrix, lacunae in small clusters, no rings.
Adipose vs. lung (simple squamous)Adipose: round, evenly sized cells packed together, nuclei pressed to the rim. Lung: irregular large air spaces separated by very thin walls.

Check Yourself

Slide identification quiz

Some questions show a microscope field. Two tries per question; the explanation appears after you answer.

Question 1
Name the tissue in this field.
Why: Nuclei sit at several different heights, cilia fringe the surface, and goblet cells are mixed in. Every cell still touches the basement membrane, so it is pseudostratified. Simple columnar has nuclei in one row and microvilli instead of cilia.
Question 2
Name the tissue in this field.
Why: Striated cells that branch, with one central nucleus each and dark stepped cross lines (intercalated discs), are cardiac muscle. Skeletal muscle would show many nuclei at the edges and no branching.
Question 3
Name the tissue in this field.
Why: Chondrocytes in lacunae tell you it is cartilage; the dense web of dark wavy fibers says elastic. Hyaline has a smooth glassy matrix with no visible fibers; fibrocartilage shows thick pink collagen bundles with cells in rows.
Question 4
Name the tissue in this field.
Why: Tightly packed, parallel, wavy collagen bundles with flat nuclei squeezed between them = dense regular (tendon). Smooth muscle cells have tapered ends and plump central nuclei; fibrocartilage has round lacunae in rows.
Question 5
Name the tissue in this field.
Why: Rings of cube-shaped cells with round central nuclei around a small lumen are kidney tubules lined with simple cuboidal epithelium.
Question 6
Name the tissue in this field.
Why: Spindle-shaped cells with tapered ends, one cigar-shaped central nucleus, and no striations are smooth muscle.
Question 7
Which feature proves a tissue is pseudostratified instead of truly stratified?
Why: Pseudostratified means "falsely layered." Only one layer of cells exists; they are different heights, so their nuclei line up at different levels. Goblet cells also occur in simple columnar epithelium.
Question 8
Which tissue lines the urinary bladder and flattens when the bladder fills?
Why: Transitional epithelium (urothelium) has dome-shaped surface cells that flatten as the organ stretches, then round up again when it empties.
Question 9
Why do adipocytes look empty on a slide?
Why: Alcohol and other solvents used to prepare the slide dissolve the lipid droplet, leaving a clear space with a thin rim of cytoplasm and a flattened nucleus at the edge.
Question 10
Where would you find fibrocartilage?
Why: Fibrocartilage resists compression in the intervertebral discs, pubic symphysis, and knee menisci. The ear is elastic cartilage; tracheal rings and articular surfaces are hyaline.
Question 11
Which epithelium is best suited for rapid diffusion of gases?
Why: One layer of flat cells gives the shortest possible distance for diffusion, which is why it lines the air sacs of the lungs and the capillaries.
Question 12
Blood counts as a connective tissue because...
Why: Like all connective tissues, blood is cells within a matrix; its matrix just happens to be fluid. Its "fibers" (fibrin) appear only when it clots.

Watch Out

Where students lose points on this lab

The mistakeThe fix
Writing just "squamous" or "columnar."Epithelium needs both words: layers + shape ("stratified squamous"). Add "keratinized/nonkeratinized" or "ciliated" when it applies.
Naming a stratified tissue by its basal cells.Always name by the surface layer. Stratified squamous has cuboidal basal cells but flat surface cells.
Calling pseudostratified "stratified columnar."Nuclei at many heights plus cilia and goblet cells = pseudostratified ciliated columnar. True stratified columnar is rare.
Mixing up dense regular CT and smooth muscle.Look at the nuclei: flat and squeezed between fibers (dense regular) vs. plump and centered inside tapered cells (smooth).
Calling every lacuna "bone."Lacunae occur in cartilage and bone. Concentric rings and canaliculi = bone; glassy matrix = cartilage.
Guessing hyaline when you see fibers.Visible dark wavy fibers = elastic cartilage; thick pink parallel bundles = fibrocartilage. Hyaline looks smooth.
Forgetting cardiac muscle is striated.Cardiac and skeletal are both striated. Use nucleus position, branching, and intercalated discs to separate them.
Mixing up dendrites and the axon.Dendrites: several short, tapering branches. Axon: one long, even-width process leaving from the pale axon hillock.
