Lab 5: Axial Skeleton — Vertebrae, Sternum, Ribs & Hyoid

This lab finishes the axial skeleton below the skull: the vertebral column, the sternum, the ribs, and the hyoid bone. On the lab practical you will be handed loose vertebrae, an articulated column, a rib, or a sternum and asked to name a part or decide which region a bone came from. This review teaches each structure with a "where it is / how to spot it" table, then drills it with numbered pins and a timed practical.

What this lab tests you on

Name the regions and curvatures of the vertebral column on an articulated model
Name the parts of a typical vertebra: body, pedicle, lamina, processes, foramina
Decide whether a loose vertebra is cervical, thoracic, or lumbar; pick out the atlas, axis, and C7
Name the features of the sacrum and coccyx, front and back
Name the parts of the sternum and a typical rib; classify ribs as true, false, or floating
Name the body and horns of the hyoid bone

By the end of this review, you will be able to:

  • 1Name the five regions of the vertebral column, how many bones each has, and the four curvatures (primary vs. secondary)
  • 2Identify every part of a typical vertebra and explain what an intervertebral disc and an intervertebral foramen are
  • 3Use the giveaway features (transverse foramina, costal facets, body size, spinous process shape) to sort any vertebra by region, and identify the atlas, axis, dens, and vertebra prominens
  • 4Identify the parts of the sternum and of a typical rib, and classify ribs 1–12
  • 5Identify the body, greater horns, and lesser horns of the hyoid bone

Your lab manual wins. Lab sections vary in which models, slides, and terms they use. If anything here differs from your lab handout or what your instructor emphasized, go with your lab material and tell your tutor so this review can be updated.

Vertebral Column

Five regions, four curvatures, and the discs between

The adult vertebral column has 26 bones: 24 separate vertebrae (7 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar) plus the sacrum and the coccyx. A memory trick for the counts: breakfast at 7, lunch at 12, dinner at 5. On the articulated model, read the column top to bottom and watch the bodies get bigger — they carry more weight the lower they sit.

The four curvatures

Seen from the side, a healthy adult column is an S-shaped spring. The two primary curves (thoracic and sacral) are left over from the C-shaped spine of a fetus. The two secondary curves (cervical and lumbar) develop after birth as the baby lifts its head and later stands.

Intervertebral disc, superior view

Intervertebral discs are pads of fibrocartilage between the bodies of neighboring vertebrae, from C2–C3 down to L5–S1 (there is no disc between C1 and C2). Each has a tough outer ring, the anulus fibrosus (many books spell it annulus; both are fine), around a soft, gel-like center, the nucleus pulposus. Discs absorb shock and let the column bend. Intervertebral foramina are the side openings between neighboring vertebrae, made by the notches above and below each pedicle; spinal nerves leave the column through them.

Sacrum and coccyx

The sacrum is five vertebrae fused into one triangle. Its broad base faces up to meet L5; its narrow apex points down to the coccyx. Turn it over to tell the surfaces apart: the anterior (pelvic) surface is smooth and concave with large foramina, and the posterior surface is rough and bumpy with a midline crest.

A Typical Vertebra

One basic plan: body in front, arch behind

Every movable vertebra below C1 follows the same plan, so learn it once. The body carries weight. Behind it, the vertebral arch (two pedicles + two laminae) wraps around the vertebral foramen, where the spinal cord runs. Seven processes stick out of the arch: one spinous process, two transverse processes, two superior articular processes, and two inferior articular processes. The articular processes link each vertebra to the ones above and below.

Model tip: to orient a loose vertebra, put the body toward you (anterior) and the spinous process away from you, then look down from above. Pedicles are the short "legs" from the body; laminae are the plates that meet at the spinous process. In a lateral view, the deep notch under the pedicle is the inferior vertebral notch — stack two vertebrae and you will see it become an intervertebral foramen.

Regional Features

Cervical, thoracic, or lumbar? The giveaway features

On the practical you will often get one loose vertebra and a single question: which region? Work through three checks in order: (1) Is there a hole in each transverse process? → cervical. (2) Are there smooth costal facets on the body and transverse processes? → thoracic. (3) Neither, and the body is huge? → lumbar. Then look for the special vertebrae: the atlas, the axis, and C7.

Atlas (superior view), axis (anterior view), and typical cervical, thoracic, and lumbar vertebrae (superior views, anterior at the top)

Atlas vs. axis on the model: the atlas is a ring with no body and no spinous process; its kidney-shaped superior facets cradle the occipital condyles. The axis is the one with the upright tooth, the dens (odontoid process), which fits into the front of the atlas ring. Together they form the atlantoaxial joint you use to shake your head "no."

Sternum, Ribs & Hyoid

The sternum, the twelve pairs of ribs, and the hyoid

The thoracic cage is the thoracic vertebrae behind, the sternum in front, and 12 pairs of ribs with their costal cartilages in between. Left and right are always the specimen's: in an anterior view, the ribs on your left side of the picture are the body's right ribs.

Ribs: classification

GroupRibsAnterior attachment
True ribs (vertebrosternal)1–7Each has its own costal cartilage that reaches the sternum

Two naming conventions. Many courses (and the KCE week 9 study guide) list true ribs 1–7, false ribs 8–10, and floating ribs 11–12. The Martini textbook calls all of ribs 8–12 false ribs, with 11–12 as the floating subgroup. Both are correct. If a station asks "true, false, or floating?" for rib 11 or 12, "floating" is the most specific answer.

Hyoid bone

The hyoid is a U-shaped bone in the front of the neck, between the mandible and the larynx. It is the only bone in the body that does not articulate with another bone; it hangs from the styloid processes of the temporal bones by ligaments and anchors muscles of the tongue and throat.

StructureWhereHow to identify it
BodyFront of the UCurved central bar, in the neck just above the larynx
Greater horns (cornua)Extending back from each end of the bodyLong, tapering arms
Lesser horns (cornua)Where the body meets each greater hornSmall cones pointing upward (attach the stylohyoid ligaments)

Check Yourself

Lab 5 review questions

Question 1
A loose vertebra has a hole in each transverse process and a short, forked spinous process. Which region?
Why: Transverse foramina occur only in cervical vertebrae, and a bifid spinous process is typical of C2–C6.
Question 2
A vertebra has a heart-shaped body, a small round vertebral foramen, and smooth facets on the sides of its body. Which region?
Why: The smooth facets are costal facets where the heads of the ribs articulate — only thoracic vertebrae have them.
Question 3
Which two curvatures are primary curves, present at birth?
Why: Thoracic and sacral curves are left from the fetal C-shape. Cervical and lumbar are secondary curves that form as the baby holds up its head and starts walking.
Question 4
The dens (odontoid process) belongs to which vertebra?
Why: The dens projects up from the body of the axis into the ring of the atlas and acts as the pivot for rotation.
Question 5
The vertebral arch is formed by:
Why: Two pedicles plus two laminae make the arch; the processes project from it.
Question 6
Spinal nerves leave the vertebral column through the:
Why: Intervertebral foramina are side openings between neighboring vertebrae, formed by the vertebral notches. The vertebral foramen holds the spinal cord itself.
Question 7
The gel-like center of an intervertebral disc is the:
Why: The nucleus pulposus is surrounded by the fibrocartilage ring, the anulus (annulus) fibrosus.
Question 8
The bumpy ridge running down the posterior midline of the sacrum is the:
Why: The median sacral crest is made of the fused spinous processes of the sacral vertebrae. The promontory and alae are on the base; transverse lines are on the anterior surface.
Question 9
The sternal angle is a landmark for finding which rib?
Why: The costal cartilage of rib 2 attaches at the sternal angle, the manubrium–body junction, so clinicians count ribs from there.
Question 10
Ribs 8–10 attach to the sternum:
Why: Ribs 8–10 are false (vertebrochondral) ribs. Ribs 1–7 attach directly; ribs 11–12 float.
Question 11
Which part of a rib articulates with the transverse costal facet of a thoracic vertebra?
Why: The head meets the vertebral body (costal facets); the tubercle meets the transverse process (transverse costal facet).
Question 12
Which statement about the hyoid is true?
Why: The hyoid is suspended by ligaments from the styloid processes of the temporal bones. Its greater horns are the long arms; the lesser horns are small cones.

Watch Out

Where students lose points on this lab

The mistakeThe fix
Calling the vertebral foramen the "intervertebral foramen" (or the reverse).Vertebral foramen = the big hole in ONE vertebra for the spinal cord. Intervertebral foramen = the side gap BETWEEN two vertebrae for a spinal nerve.
Mixing up the transverse foramen with the transverse costal facet.Foramen = a hole (cervical). Facet = a smooth joint surface (thoracic, for the rib tubercle).
Calling a big thoracic vertebra "lumbar."Check for costal facets first. Any facet on the body = thoracic, no matter the size.
Swapping pedicle and lamina.Pedicles attach to the body (think "foot"); laminae are the plates that meet behind at the spinous process.
Swapping atlas and axis.Atlas = ring, no body. Axis = the one with the dens sticking up.
Reading the sacrum upside down or backward.The wide base goes up. The smooth concave side with big foramina is anterior; the bumpy crest is posterior.
Calling the sternal angle the "xiphoid" or the jugular notch the "sternal angle."Jugular notch = top of the manubrium. Sternal angle = manubrium–body ridge (rib 2). Xiphoid = bottom tip.
Mixing up the rib's head and tubercle.Head = the very end, meets the vertebral body. Tubercle = the knob a little farther out, meets the transverse process.
Reading left and right from your own point of view.Left and right always mean the specimen's. In an anterior view the specimen's right is on your left.
