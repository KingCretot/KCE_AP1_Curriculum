The Appendicular Skeleton

Last week's axial skeleton protected the body's core. This week's appendicular skeleton — the limbs and their girdles — is built for an entirely different job: movement. You'll see a clear structural trade-off between mobility and stability play out across four regions.

By the end of this week, you will be able to:

  • 1Identify the four major regions of the appendicular skeleton
  • 2Name the bones of the upper limb, proximal to distal
  • 3Name the bones of the lower limb, proximal to distal
  • 4Compare the pectoral and pelvic girdles
  • 5Explain the mobility-vs-stability trade-off across the appendicular skeleton
  • 6Recognize common fracture sites in the appendicular skeleton

Big Picture

Four regions, one repeating pattern

The appendicular skeleton's 126 bones organize into four groups that repeat a single pattern twice: a girdle anchors a limb to the axial skeleton, then the limb extends outward in a proximal-to-distal sequence. Learn the pattern once, and the upper and lower limb both make sense.

Vocabulary & Word Parts

Appendicular skeleton roots

append/o-attachment, appendage
Appendicular skeleton = the bones "attached to" the axial skeleton — limbs and girdles.
brachi/o-arm
Brachial refers to the arm; the humerus is sometimes called the "brachium" bone.
carp/o-wrist
Carpals = the 8 small wrist bones.
tars/o-ankle
Tarsals = the 7 ankle bones — the lower-limb equivalent of carpals.
phalan/o-finger/toe bone
Phalanges = the bones of the fingers and toes, 14 in each hand and each foot.
pelv/o-basin, pelvis
Pelvic girdle = the hip bones, shaped like a basin that supports the trunk's weight.

Structure & Function

Upper limb: proximal to distal

One bone becomes two, then many, then more: a single humerus in the arm gives way to a paired radius (thumb-side) and ulna (pinky-side) in the forearm, then the 8 carpals, 5 metacarpals, and 14 phalanges of the wrist and hand — built for fine, precise movement.

Structure & Function

Lower limb: proximal to distal

The lower limb mirrors the upper limb's pattern almost bone-for-bone: femur parallels humerus, tibia/fibula parallel radius/ulna, tarsals parallel carpals. The key differences reflect the lower limb's job — weight-bearing and stability instead of fine manipulation. The patella (kneecap) has no upper-limb equivalent.

Structure & Function

Pectoral vs. pelvic girdle: a mobility/stability trade-off

This is the single biggest conceptual takeaway for this week: the pectoral girdle is loosely attached to the axial skeleton, trading stability for an enormous range of arm motion — which is also exactly why shoulder dislocations are so common. The pelvic girdle is firmly fused to the sacrum, trading mobility for the stability needed to support body weight and permit walking.

Your Turn — Guided Practice

Apply it before the quiz

A patient falls onto an outstretched hand and reports pain in the forearm on the thumb side, near the wrist.

Question 1 — Which forearm bone is most likely fractured?

The radius sits on the thumb side of the forearm — exactly matching the pain location described. This injury pattern (fall onto an outstretched hand, thumb-side pain) is common enough clinically to have its own name: a distal radius fracture.

Question 2 — Is this bone part of the axial or appendicular skeleton?

The radius is part of the upper limb, making it appendicular. Only the skull, vertebral column, and thoracic cage belong to the axial skeleton — everything in the limbs and girdles is appendicular.

Clinical Correlation

Why shoulder dislocations are so common

This is a direct, real-world consequence of the pectoral girdle's mobility-over-stability design.

🩺 In practice

The shoulder joint is the most frequently dislocated joint in the body — a direct trade-off for the wide range of motion the loosely-attached pectoral girdle allows. Compare this to the hip joint (part of the firmly-anchored pelvic girdle), which dislocates far less often precisely because it sacrifices some mobility for structural stability. Same basic joint concept, opposite design priorities.

Clinical Correlation

Hip fractures and the elderly

This connects the pelvic girdle's weight-bearing role directly to a major real-world health concern.

🩺 In practice

Because the femur and pelvic girdle bear so much of the body's weight, a hip fracture (typically at the proximal femur, near the pelvic girdle) is a serious injury in older adults — often linked to osteoporosis from Chapter 6, since weakened bone fractures more easily under normal walking loads. This is exactly why fall prevention and bone density screening are emphasized so heavily for aging patients.

Mini Knowledge Test

Show what you know — 7 questions

Question 1
Which bone is part of the pectoral girdle?
The clavicle (collarbone), along with the scapula, makes up the pectoral girdle. The femur is a lower-limb bone, and the sacrum is part of the axial skeleton.
Question 2
Which forearm bone is on the pinky side?
The ulna runs along the pinky side of the forearm. The radius is on the thumb side instead, and the humerus is the single upper-arm bone above the elbow.
Question 3
How many carpal bones are in each wrist?
There are 8 carpal bones per wrist. 5 is the number of metacarpals in the palm, and 14 is the number of phalanges in the fingers.
Question 4
The patella (kneecap) has no direct equivalent in the:
The patella is unique to the lower limb — there's no equivalent bone at the elbow in the upper limb. It's part of the lower limb and unrelated to the pelvic girdle's own structure.
Question 5
Which girdle is more firmly attached to the axial skeleton?
The pelvic girdle is firmly fused to the sacrum for stability and weight-bearing. The pectoral girdle is loosely attached instead, trading stability for a wide range of arm motion.
Question 6
Which region of the appendicular skeleton is built primarily for weight-bearing and stability rather than fine movement?
The lower limb and pelvic girdle are built for weight-bearing and stability, supporting the body during standing and walking. The upper limb and pectoral girdle instead prioritize mobility and fine manipulation.
Question 7
A distal radius fracture from falling on an outstretched hand affects which skeletal division?
The radius is part of the upper limb, which belongs entirely to the appendicular skeleton. The axial skeleton (skull, vertebral column, thoracic cage) is unaffected by this type of injury.

Week 10 Quick-Reference Recap

One page to glance at before Exam 3 — everything from this week, condensed.

Four Regions

  • Pectoral girdle — clavicle, scapula
  • Upper limb — humerus to fingers
  • Pelvic girdle — hip bones
  • Lower limb — femur to toes

Upper Limb Sequence

Humerus → Radius/Ulna → Carpals (8) → Metacarpals (5) → Phalanges (14).

Lower Limb Sequence

Femur → Patella → Tibia/Fibula → Tarsals (7) → Metatarsals (5) → Phalanges (14).

Girdle Trade-off

  • Pectoral — mobile, less stable
  • Pelvic — stable, less mobile

Common Injuries

Shoulder dislocation (pectoral girdle mobility), hip fracture (pelvic girdle/femur, often osteoporosis-linked).
