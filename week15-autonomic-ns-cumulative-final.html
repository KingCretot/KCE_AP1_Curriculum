Part 1 — Big Picture

Somatic vs. autonomic: two halves of the motor system

The motor side of the nervous system splits into two systems. The somatic nervous system gives voluntary control over skeletal muscle, using a single neuron running straight from the CNS to the muscle. The autonomic nervous system (ANS) instead gives involuntary control over organs, glands, and blood vessels, always using two neurons in series with a ganglion in between.

Part 1 — Vocabulary & Word Parts

Autonomic nervous system roots

auto-self
Autonomic = "self-governing" — runs without conscious control.
sympath/o-sympathy, feeling with
Sympathetic division = the "fight or flight" branch, active under stress.
para-beside, alongside
Parasympathetic division = literally "alongside" the sympathetic, the "rest and digest" branch.
gangli/o-ganglion, knot
A ganglion is a cluster of neuron cell bodies outside the CNS — where pre- and postganglionic neurons connect.
chol/o- / -ergicrelating to a neurotransmitter
Cholinergic = signaling with acetylcholine, the parasympathetic neurotransmitter.
adren/o-adrenal gland, epinephrine
Adrenergic = signaling with norepinephrine/epinephrine, the sympathetic neurotransmitter family.

Part 1 — Structure & Function

Sympathetic vs. parasympathetic: opposite effects, same organs

Most organs receive input from both divisions, which push in opposite directions to keep the body balanced. The sympathetic division (norepinephrine) mobilizes the body for action: heart rate rises, airways widen, and digestion is put on hold. The parasympathetic division (acetylcholine) does the opposite, conserving energy and supporting digestion during calm conditions.

Part 1 — Structure & Function

The two-neuron autonomic pathway

Unlike the single-neuron somatic pathway, every autonomic signal travels through exactly two neurons: a preganglionic neuron runs from the CNS to a ganglion, and a postganglionic neuron continues from that ganglion to the target organ. This two-neuron relay, with its built-in ganglion checkpoint, is the structural signature of the entire autonomic system.

Part 1 — Clinical Correlation

Epinephrine and anaphylaxis: the sympathetic response, delivered by injection

This connects the sympathetic effects above directly to a medication many students already recognize.

🩺 In practice

An EpiPen delivers epinephrine, a hormone that mimics norepinephrine's sympathetic effects almost exactly. During anaphylaxis (a severe allergic reaction), airways can swell shut and blood pressure can crash; injected epinephrine rapidly reverses both by triggering the same "fight or flight" effects covered above — opening airways and constricting blood vessels to raise blood pressure — buying critical time before emergency care arrives.

Part 2 — Cross-Chapter Connections

Three threads that run through the entire course

Thread 1 — Structure predicts function, at every scale. A bone's shape predicts its joint type (Ch. 7-9); a muscle's shape and attachment points predict its action (Ch. 10-11); a neuron's shape predicts its role (Ch. 12). This single idea, first introduced with bone, explains structures in every system covered since.

Thread 2 — The microscopic explains the macroscopic. Sliding filaments (invisible) explain whole-muscle contraction (visible); individual neurons (invisible) explain entire nervous-system pathways (whole-body). Zooming in always explains what you see when you zoom back out.

Thread 3 — The spine is the throughline. Vertebrae (Ch. 7) protect the spinal cord (Ch. 13), which routes signals to every muscle (Ch. 10-11) and organ (Ch. 16) in the body — one structure, first introduced in Exam Block 3, that reappears in every block after it.

Your Turn — One Final Application

Bring it all together before the quiz

A person is startled by a loud noise: their heart rate spikes, pupils dilate, and they feel ready to run.

Question 1 — Which autonomic division is responsible for this response?

This is the classic sympathetic, "fight or flight" response — heart rate rising and pupils dilating are textbook sympathetic effects, mobilizing the body for immediate action.

Question 2 — Structurally, how did this signal reach the heart?

Every autonomic signal — sympathetic or parasympathetic — travels through two neurons connected at a ganglion. A single-neuron pathway describes the somatic system instead, which controls skeletal muscle, not the heart.

Question 1
The chemical level of organization (Ch. 2) is foundational because:

Question 2

Question 3
A bone's shape (long, flat, irregular) most directly predicts its:
Bone shape predicts function and joint compatibility — the same structure-predicts-function thread traced across the whole course.
Question 4
The sliding filament theory (Ch. 10) explains contraction at the level of the:
Sliding filaments act at the sarcomere level — the microscopic mechanism that, scaled up, produces visible whole-muscle contraction.
Question 5
A muscle named using the "action + location" convention (Ch. 11) would be exemplified by:
"Flexor carpi radialis" combines action (flexor) with location (carpi radialis, the wrist). "Maximus" names by size; "deltoid" names by shape.
Question 6
Which neuroglial cell myelinates axons in the CNS?
Oligodendrocytes myelinate CNS axons. Schwann cells do the same job in the PNS; astrocytes provide general neuron support instead.
Question 7
In a spinal cord cross-section, the gray matter is located:
Spinal cord gray matter sits in an inner, H-shaped core, surrounded by white matter — the reverse of the brain's organization.
Question 8
Which cerebral lobe processes vision?
The occipital lobe, at the back of the brain, processes vision.
Question 9
Upper motor neuron damage typically produces:
Upper motor neuron damage produces spastic paralysis. Flaccid paralysis with absent reflexes instead points to lower motor neuron damage.
Question 10
The parasympathetic division's neurotransmitter is:
The parasympathetic division signals with acetylcholine. The sympathetic division uses norepinephrine instead; calcium's role is in muscle contraction (Ch. 10), not autonomic signaling.
Question 11
Every autonomic pathway, sympathetic or parasympathetic, uses:
Autonomic pathways always use exactly two neurons in series, meeting at a ganglion. The single-neuron pathway belongs to the somatic system instead.
Question 12
Across the whole course, the idea that shows up most consistently, from bone to muscle to neuron, is that:
Structure predicts function is the thread traced across every exam block this semester — from bone shape to muscle attachment to neuron shape, the same principle keeps reappearing.
