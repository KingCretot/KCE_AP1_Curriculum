The Cellular Level of Organization

The cell is the smallest living unit of the body — everything from the phospholipids and proteins you studied last week now assembles into a working, self-sustaining structure. This week covers the membrane, the major organelles, how material moves in and out of the cell, and the basic cell life cycle.

By the end of this week, you will be able to:

  • 1Describe the plasma membrane's structure and explain selective permeability
  • 2Identify the major organelles and their functions
  • 3Compare passive and active transport mechanisms
  • 4Explain osmosis and predict what happens to a cell in different tonicity solutions
  • 5Outline the basic stages of the cell life cycle and mitosis

Big Picture

The cell is a fully staffed factory, not a simple bag

It's tempting to picture a cell as a blob with a nucleus in the middle. In reality, it's closer to a factory with dedicated departments: a control center (nucleus), a manufacturing floor (ER and ribosomes), a shipping department (Golgi apparatus), a power plant (mitochondria), and a security perimeter (the plasma membrane) that decides exactly what gets in and out.

Every organelle you learn this week has ONE job, and that job connects directly back to something you already know: the membrane is built from the phospholipids of Week 2; the proteins made on ribosomes are built from the amino acids of Week 2; the ATP produced by mitochondria is the same ATP you previewed last week.

Vocabulary & Word Parts

Cell biology roots

cyto-cell
Cytoplasm = the fluid filling the cell. Cytoskeleton = the cell's internal scaffolding.
-somebody
Ribosome = a small "body" that builds proteins. Lysosome = a "body" full of digestive enzymes.
endo- / exo-into / out of
Endocytosis = bringing material INTO the cell. Exocytosis = pushing material OUT.
phago- / pino-eating / drinking
Phagocytosis = "cell eating" solids. Pinocytosis = "cell drinking" fluid.
mito-thread
Mitochondrion (named for its thread-like appearance under early microscopes) — the organelle that produces ATP.
-osisa process or condition
Mitosis = the process of cell division. Necrosis = the condition of cell death.

Structure & Function

The plasma membrane: a selectively permeable gate

The phospholipid bilayer (from Week 2) forms the membrane's basic structure — heads facing the watery environment on both sides, tails hiding in the middle. Embedded proteins act as channels, carriers, receptors, and identification markers, controlling exactly what crosses.
  • Phospholipid heads face water on both sides
  • Phospholipid tails hide from water in the middle
  • Membrane proteins control what passes through

Meet the organelles

Every organelle has a specialized job. This is the map you'll keep referring back to as each one is introduced individually on the next few slides.

Structure & Function

The nucleus, ER, and ribosomes: the manufacturing chain

Protein production is a coordinated, multi-step process — not something any single organelle does alone.
1
1 · Nucleus
Houses the DNA — the instructions for every protein the cell can build. The nuclear envelope controls what enters and exits.
2
2 · Ribosomes
Read the genetic instructions and assemble amino acids into proteins. Some float free in the cytoplasm; others attach to the ER.
3
3 · Rough ER
Studded with ribosomes (hence "rough") — this is where proteins destined for export or the membrane get built and folded.
4
4 · Smooth ER
No ribosomes (hence "smooth") — builds lipids and helps detoxify certain chemicals, rather than making proteins.
Notice the pattern: nucleus gives instructions, ribosomes build, rough ER packages what's being built for export — this is the same logical chain you'll see repeated in the next slide's shipping step.

Structure & Function

The Golgi apparatus: the shipping department

The Golgi apparatus receives proteins from the rough ER, modifies them further (adding tags, folding them into final shape), and packages them into vesicles addressed for their final destination — whether that's the plasma membrane, a lysosome, or straight out of the cell entirely via exocytosis.

Assembly-line analogy: nucleus = blueprint office → ribosome = assembly floor → rough ER = quality control → Golgi = shipping and labeling → vesicle = delivery truck.

Structure & Function

Mitochondria, lysosomes, and the cytoskeleton

  • Mitochondria — the cell's power plant. This is where most of the cell's ATP is produced through cellular respiration, using oxygen and glucose. Cells that need a lot of energy (like muscle and liver cells) are packed with far more mitochondria than cells that don't.
  • Lysosomes — contain digestive enzymes that break down worn-out organelles, debris, and (in white blood cells) engulfed pathogens. Think of them as the cell's recycling and waste-disposal unit.
  • Cytoskeleton — a network of protein filaments that gives the cell its shape, allows organelles to be transported internally, and (in some cells) allows movement.
  • Centrioles — organize the spindle fibers that pull chromosomes apart during cell division, which you'll see later this week.

Structure & Function

Three ways material crosses the membrane

Notice the key distinguishing question for each: does it need a protein channel, and does it need ATP energy? Those two questions sort almost every transport mechanism you'll encounter this semester.

Structure & Function

Osmosis and tonicity

Osmosis is a special case of diffusion — specifically the movement of WATER across a selectively permeable membrane, from an area of lower solute concentration to an area of higher solute concentration. Tonicity describes how a solution's solute concentration compares to the inside of the cell.

Structure & Function

Vesicular transport: moving bulk material

Some material is too large to pass through channels or carriers one molecule at a time — it has to be moved in bulk, wrapped in a membrane-bound sac called a vesicle.

Endocytosis brings material INTO the cell by pinching off a piece of the plasma membrane around it — phagocytosis for solid particles, pinocytosis for fluid. Exocytosis works in reverse, fusing a vesicle with the plasma membrane to release its contents outside the cell — this is exactly how the Golgi's packaged proteins actually get out.

Both directions require ATP — moving large material by wrapping and unwrapping membrane is active work, not passive diffusion.

Structure & Function

The cell life cycle: interphase and mitosis

A cell spends most of its life in interphase — growing, functioning, and copying its DNA in preparation for division. Mitosis is the shorter, active division phase: chromosomes condense (prophase), line up (metaphase), separate (anaphase), and two new nuclei form (telophase), producing two genetically identical daughter cells.

Your Turn — Guided Practice

Apply it before the quiz

A red blood cell is placed into a solution and, under the microscope, begins to visibly swell.

Question 1 — What kind of solution was the cell most likely placed into?

A cell swells when water moves INTO it — that happens when the surrounding solution has a LOWER solute concentration than the cell's interior, which is the definition of a hypotonic solution. A hypertonic solution would pull water OUT of the cell, causing it to shrink instead.

Question 2 — Is water movement into the cell here an example of active transport or passive transport?

Osmosis is water moving down its own concentration gradient (from more water/less solute to less water/more solute) — that's a passive process, requiring no ATP. Active transport is reserved for moving substances AGAINST their concentration gradient, which isn't what's happening here.

Clinical Correlation

Cystic fibrosis: a membrane channel defect

A single malfunctioning membrane protein can cause a serious systemic disease — a direct, high-stakes example of why membrane transport matters clinically.

🩺 In practice

Cystic fibrosis is caused by a defective chloride channel protein (CFTR) in the plasma membrane. Without properly functioning chloride channels, water doesn't follow chloride ions the way it should across cell membranes, resulting in abnormally thick, sticky mucus in the lungs and digestive tract — all traced back to a single faulty membrane transport protein.

Clinical Correlation

Why IV fluid tonicity is a life-or-death detail

This directly follows up on Week 2's IV fluid preview — now you have the full cellular picture of why it matters.

🩺 In practice

Infusing a hypotonic IV solution too quickly can cause red blood cells to swell and potentially burst (hemolysis) as water rushes in by osmosis. Infusing a hypertonic solution can cause cells to shrink (crenation) and malfunction. This is exactly why nurses and pharmacists are trained to double-check IV fluid tonicity before administration — it's cellular transport with direct patient-safety consequences.

Mini Knowledge Test

Show what you know — 7 questions

Question 1
What forms the basic structure of the plasma membrane?
The membrane's basic structure is a phospholipid bilayer — two layers of phospholipids with heads facing the watery environment and tails hiding in the middle. Proteins are embedded WITHIN this bilayer, not the base structure itself.
Question 2
Which organelle is studded with ribosomes and involved in protein export?
The rough ER gets its name specifically from the ribosomes studding its surface, and it's where proteins destined for export or the membrane are built. The smooth ER has no ribosomes and focuses on lipid synthesis instead; the Golgi apparatus modifies and packages proteins AFTER the rough ER, not during initial synthesis.
Question 3
Which organelle produces most of the cell's ATP?
Mitochondria are the cell's power plants, producing ATP through cellular respiration. Lysosomes handle digestion/waste breakdown, and the Golgi apparatus handles protein packaging — neither produces significant ATP.
Question 4
Moving a substance against its concentration gradient requires:
Moving something AGAINST its gradient (low to high concentration) always requires active transport, which costs ATP energy. Simple diffusion only ever moves substances WITH their gradient (high to low), which requires no added energy.
Question 5
A cell placed in a hypertonic solution will:
"Hyper"tonic means the surrounding solution has a HIGHER solute concentration than the cell — water follows its own concentration gradient and moves OUT of the cell toward the more concentrated solution, causing the cell to shrink (crenation).
Question 6
Phagocytosis is best described as:
Phago- means "eating" — phagocytosis is a cell engulfing a SOLID particle (like a white blood cell engulfing a bacterium). Engulfing fluid instead is pinocytosis ("drinking"). Neither involves a simple channel opening — both are bulk vesicular transport processes.
Question 7
During which phase of mitosis do chromosomes line up at the center of the cell?
Metaphase is specifically the lining-up-in-the-middle phase — a useful memory anchor is that the chromosomes are in the "middle" during meta-phase. Prophase is the earlier condensing stage; anaphase is when chromosomes are actively pulled apart to opposite ends.

Plasma Membrane

Phospholipid bilayer + embedded proteins = selective permeability.

Key Organelles

  • Nucleus — DNA, control center
  • Ribosomes — build proteins
  • Rough/Smooth ER — protein/lipid synthesis
  • Golgi — modify, package, ship
  • Mitochondria — ATP production
  • Lysosomes — digestion/waste

Passive Transport

  • Diffusion — high → low, no protein needed
  • Facilitated diffusion — high → low, needs a channel
  • Osmosis — water only, follows solute gradient

Active Transport

Low → high concentration, always requires ATP.

Tonicity

  • Hypertonic — cell shrinks
  • Isotonic — no change
  • Hypotonic — cell swells

Cell Cycle

Interphase (grow + copy DNA) → Prophase → Metaphase → Anaphase → Telophase (mitosis)
